Does a Vaani re‑search after a decision turn increase same‑category purchase?

When a user is negotiating price — or otherwise deciding — and Vaani responds by searching an alternate product, does that raise the chance of a cart‑add or order from the same sub‑category, in that same session, versus no search happening at all?

4,144,272 turns 978,529 sessions 103,095 sessions ground-truthed via Metabase 5 decide cohorts × 4 search treatments
39.0%→~31%
Same‑category conversion, price negotiation — no search vs. Vaani searched. Every cohort tested moves the same direction.
43–47%→33–37%
Share of different‑product purchases found via the PDP recommendation feed — before vs. after Vaani starts searching instead.

Two separate questions, two different answers: searching doesn’t convert better (stat 1), but it does displace generic recommendations with its own results once it fires (stat 2). See §3 and §7.2.

§1

Data & method

Source: all_turns_flat_consolidated_with_outcome.jsonl — delivered directly, not a Metabase pull. Every conversational turn is pre‑tagged FIND / DECIDE / OTHERS, and carries a pre‑computed, forward‑looking outcome: what happened next in the session — a re‑search, a click, a cart‑add/order, or nothing. Pure local computation, full population, ~40 seconds to process.

Cohorts — the decision being made

price_negotiation
DECIDE/price turns tagged as negotiation in the earlier granular file (joined on session+turn; 100% concordant with this file’s own tag on a 3,000‑row sample)
price_other
DECIDE/price turns that aren’t negotiation — lookup, discount ask, final‑amount confirmation, mismatch complaint, comparison
quality · how_to_use
DECIDE turns tagged as such directly
other_decide
other_product_information, delivery_time, size_fit, quantity_pack_contents, comparison

Treatment — what Vaani did next

Scanning forward through the session’s own turn sequence for the first turn where Vaani actually rendered a search:

vaani_search_same_sscat
a search happened, and its resulting category matches the sscat of the product being decided on
vaani_search_diff_sscat
a search happened, different category
vaani_search_unknown_sscat
a search happened but the category on either side couldn’t be resolved
no_vaani_search
no Vaani‑rendered search turn anywhere later in the session

The sscat of “the product being decided on” isn’t populated on most rows directly, so it’s resolved by carrying forward/back the nearest known category within the session — a session generally stays on one product until a search or click changes it.

Outcome

Taken directly from the decide turn’s own pre‑computed outcome. For DECIDE turns this file’s outcome tree is a clean binary — purchase_activity vs. no_purchase_activity — with same‑product / different‑product and same‑sscat / different‑sscat already resolved by the source pipeline.

Reading it straight

In every single cohort, no_vaani_search has the highest same‑sscat purchase rate. For price negotiation specifically — the case at the center of this memo — a Vaani search afterward (same sscat or not) is associated with an ~8‑point drop in same‑sscat conversion (39.0% → ~31%), not a lift.

For completeness, “bought anything” (any sscat) tells a related but distinct story — here vaani_search_diff_sscat actually wins, and vaani_search_same_sscat is the worst of the three:

So: showing the user something in the same category they just failed to close on is the single worst‑performing move in this data — worse than doing nothing, and worse than pivoting them to something genuinely different.

Same-product conversion collapses once Vaani searches (price_negotiation: 34.0% → 20.8–23.7%) — the “interrupted a working conversation” effect from §4. But different-product-same-sscat conversion rises, roughly doubling specifically under vaani_search_same_sscat (5.0% → 10.0% for price_negotiation, 4.9% → 8.9% for price_other, and similarly elsewhere). So a same‑sscat search does genuinely help more people find a different item in that category — it just isn’t nearly enough to offset how many fewer people close on the product they were already looking at.

§4

Interpretation: why this likely isn’t “search hurts”

This is correlational data with a strong, plausible selection effect running in the direction that explains the result — read it as “search doesn’t rescue a stalling decision,” not as “search causes users to not buy”:

  • no_vaani_search is dominated by the smooth-conversion path. The default, most common negotiation arc is anchor → counter → converge → “Buy Now” nudge, all on the same product, with no search involved at all. Every one of those successful, uneventful conversions lands in no_vaani_search and counts as a trivial same‑sscat (same‑product) purchase. That cohort isn’t a neutral “did nothing” baseline — it’s disproportionately the population that was already going to buy.
  • A re-search is a symptom that the current thread failed or got redirected, not a proactively helpful nudge — either the negotiation broke down and Vaani/the user moved on, or the user simply changed their mind mid‑conversation and asked for something else. Both are markers of a harder‑to‑convert moment, not a random draw. Comparing these two populations head‑to‑head confounds “search happened” with “this particular thread was already going worse.”
  • The diff‑sscat‑search “any purchase” win (§3) is the most informative secondary read: a real category pivot (not just an alternate item in the same struggling category) recovers some purchase more often than standing pat in the same category — consistent with “the user wanted something else entirely” being a common reason these threads redirect in the first place.
The “suggest an alternate product after price negotiation, in the same sscat” pattern does not show up as a good‑outcome driver in this data — if anything it marks the weaker half of price‑negotiation sessions. This is worth a real A/B rather than inferring causality either way from this cut.
§5

Secondary finding: Vaani rarely carries the discussed constraint into its re-search

Each FIND turn already carries structured attribute_types (e.g. price_budget, quality_tier, size, quantity_pack, use_case) — so whether a post‑decision re‑search actually preserved the thing being discussed is directly checkable, no keyword‑guessing needed:

In roughly 85–95% of cases, when Vaani does search after a decision turn, that search does not carry forward the specific thing the user was just discussing. Combined with §3–4, this reframes the intervention tested: it’s less “does a constrained alternate‑product search help” and more “Vaani’s re‑searches are mostly not constrained in the first place.” A cleaner future test: restrict the treatment group to the ~10–15% where the constraint did carry over, and compare that narrower slice against both no_vaani_search and unconstrained re‑search.

§6

Caveats & limitations

  • Correlational, not causal — see §4. This table cannot tell you what would happen if you forced Vaani to search more or less; it tells you what currently co‑occurs, and there’s a clear, plausible selection story for why the naive causal reading is wrong.
  • sscat resolution is a within-session fill, not a direct field. Only ~14–65% of rows carry reference_sscat/compared_sscat directly (0% for no_purchase_activity and purchase_activity/same_product, 90%+ for search/click outcomes). Missing values are filled from the nearest known category earlier/later in the same session. vaani_search_unknown_sscat (7–14% of turns with a search) is where this still failed — reported separately, not folded into either bucket.
  • The 90,832 “price” rows not covered by the earlier granular export default into price_other rather than being confirmed non‑negotiation — likely a pull‑window mismatch between files. Doesn’t affect the main matrix, only the negotiation split’s precision at the margin.
  • “Search happened after” is turn‑sequence‑based, not strictly timestamp‑bounded against the purchase for the different‑product case (same‑product purchases do have an exact ATC/order timestamp). turn_seq is chronological, so this is a reasonable proxy, not a hard guarantee.
  • Constraint‑match (§5) uses the FIND taxonomy’s own attribute_types — a real structured tag, not inferred here — but only as accurate as the underlying classifier, not independently re‑verified in this pass.
  • §7.2’s real estate takes the first cart/order event chronologically after each decide turn as the attributed purchase. In sessions with several different‑product purchases close together this is reasonable but not certain. Coverage is otherwise complete: only 261 of 414,653 purchase‑outcome turns (0.06%) had no matching event.
verified · 30 Jul 2026

Important negative result, kept for the record. Attempted to re‑verify §3’s same‑sscat conversion numbers using clicks_json’s catalog_name field as “the sscat the user is currently on,” the same way ground truth fixed §7’s real‑estate attribution. This does not work, and was not applied. Checked catalog_name against cart_json/orders_json’s sscat_name for the same product_id across 200,002 purchase events: they agree only 8.7% of the time — catalog_name is a finer‑grained listing title (e.g. “Men Cotton Blend Oversize Tshirts”), not the canonical sub‑category. Comparing them would have silently reclassified ~55% of the 414,653 purchase‑outcome turns’ same/different‑sscat label, almost all of it spurious. §3’s original labels come from a decide_pid=X sscat='Y' resolution already in the source file, and Y correctly matches the sscat_name vocabulary (spot‑checked directly against ground truth). Conclusion: §3’s conversion table was already correct and did not need, and must not receive, a catalog_name‑based “fix.”

When Vaani doesn’t search, pdp_recommendations is the single largest discovery path for a different‑product purchase — 43–47% of them, roughly double catalog_search_results (19–22%). The moment Vaani does search, the two swap ranking: catalog_search_results becomes dominant (46–51%) and pdp_recommendations’s share drops by 9–13 points (to 33–37%). Vaani searching doesn’t eliminate recommendation‑feed discovery, but it visibly displaces a meaningful share of it with search‑driven discovery instead — users are relying on the PDP recommendation carousel to find their way to something to buy specifically because Vaani didn’t offer them an alternative directly.

widget (a less‑specific carousel placement, ~7–10% throughout, roughly flat) and smaller surfaces (main/home feed, wishlist, single_product, visual_search_results, each 1–8%) round out the different‑product mix and don’t materially shift between treatments — the swap is specifically between recommendations and search.

7.3  Read together with §3–4

This doesn’t reverse the §3 headline (searching still doesn’t lift the same‑sscat conversion rate, for the selection reasons in §4) — but it does explain how the two paths differ mechanically:

No-search sessions that still convert on a different product are substituting Vaani’s silence with the app’s own recommendation surface; search-triggered sessions convert through the search Vaani actually ran.

If the goal is to reduce reliance on generic recommendations and make Vaani itself the reason a different product gets found, this data says that’s already happening exactly where you’d expect it to — it just doesn’t (yet) come with a same‑category conversion lift on top.
