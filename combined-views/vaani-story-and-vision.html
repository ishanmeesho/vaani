Vaani · product strategy · August 2026

What Vaani is, and what it should be

We built a voice assistant that helps people use the Meesho app. It works — for one narrow job, for a small number of people, and then it stops.

Everything we have shipped so far makes the app easier to operate. None of it makes a shopper discover what an intelligent assistant could actually do for them. We have been asking people to trust Vaani before Vaani has shown them anything. This document is in three parts: what we built and what the data says about it, the shift we think comes next, and the one thing we are building now to find out if we are right.

Part I

Where we are

The bet we made, the product it produced, how people use it, and the point at which it stops growing. Every number here comes from a dashboard linked at the end.

01The bet we made

Vaani would be voice-only, and nudges would do the work of teaching people what it was.

The original approach was deliberate. Keep Vaani to a single modality — voice. Keep UX intervention minimal. Drive adoption with nudges. Because Vaani lived inside the app as one small feature rather than being the thing the app was built around, every outcome it could serve had to land on a screen the app already had. So we pointed it at problems we already knew about: usability gaps and funnel drop-offs.

Three capabilities came out of that: start a search, filter or refine a feed, and answer questions about the product on screen. Alongside them we built an adoption machine — a floating mic button, page-intro nudges on PDP, CLP and cart, and later zero-click-session nudges that fire when a session is about to end with nothing clicked.

One thing we did not expect. A new modality surfaced demand that would never have appeared in the old construct. The clearest case is price negotiation — people started haggling with Vaani, at volume, because talking made it possible to. That is the first real evidence that new ways to engage produce new opportunities, and it is the seed of Part II.

The limit of the frame — Vaani can make the existing app easier to use. It cannot easily do things the existing app was never designed to do.

Why this matters

Every finding in the rest of Part I is a downstream consequence of that one sentence. We did not build a bad assistant. We built a good assistant inside a frame that caps what it is allowed to be.

02What people actually say to Vaani

Vaani has been adopted for exactly one job: arguing about the price of the product already on screen.

We tagged every turn in a two-month window — 2,201,813 turns across 519,160 sessions and 167,082 users. Roughly six in ten turns are someone asking to see something (FIND). Three in ten are someone asking about the product in hand (DECIDE). The rest is noise, mostly speech recognition failures.

Open up the DECIDE branch and the concentration is extreme. Price is 64% of every DECIDE turn — 421,135 of them — which makes it about 19% of everything anyone says to Vaani. Separately, 44.6% of those price turns are outright negotiation: make it cheaper.

Now look at the bottom of the same chart. Turns where a user asked Vaani to compare two products: 556. Out of 2.2 million. That is not a small feature with low usage. It is a capability that effectively does not exist in the product, in the exact place where a shopper most needs help.

The habit hardens with use. Between a user's first Vaani session and their tenth, DECIDE grows from 27.3% to about 32% of turns, and price grows from 60.0% to 66.5% of DECIDE. Power users skip the learning curve entirely — price is already 64.6% of their DECIDE turns in session one.

Insight — returning users do not search more. They negotiate more. The product taught them that Vaani is a price tool, and they learned it.

Implication

The single job Vaani owns is a low-ceiling one. The job with the most latent value — helping someone decide between options — is being asked for 556 times, because nothing in the product has ever suggested Vaani could do it.

03What comes back

When Vaani searches, it works. When Vaani talks, it mostly doesn't.

Across all 2.2M turns the overall good-outcome rate is 44.7%. That average hides a split so clean it is almost a product spec.

Read from the user's side and the same split appears. A FIND ask with a category and an attribute — "cotton kurti under 500" — succeeds 68.7% of the time. A vague ask with no category succeeds 18.6% of the time. And the ask that dominates the product, DECIDE about price, succeeds 29.6% of the time.

Insight — Vaani is good at fetching and weak at reasoning out loud. Our biggest ask by volume is the one we are worst at answering.

Implication

Answering a price question with words is a dead end — 375,755 turns of specific price answers convert at 31.6%. The ask underneath it is not "what is the price". It is "should I buy this one". That is Part III.

04Trust is the hinge, and it is measurable

Whether the first answer was any good decides whether there is ever a second conversation.

We took every user's first turn of a given ask type, graded that first outcome good or bad, and then checked whether they ever came back and asked the same thing again. For DECIDE asks the effect is large and consistent.

The same pattern shows up in the nudge experiment from a different angle. Of users whose first nudged conversation ended with them clicking Vaani's own result, 30.0% came back to Vaani organically later. Of those who gave up and typed their own search instead, 20.5% came back.

FIND is different, and worth knowing: for vague FIND asks the signal inverts. Users whose first vague ask went badly come back and ask again more often, because they are still looking for the thing they never found. FIND repeat is a shopper-intent signal. DECIDE repeat is a Vaani-quality signal.

Insight — a good outcome buys the next conversation. A bad one costs it. Trust is not a soft word here; it is the mechanism.
05Where Vaani gets used

Usage collapses onto two screens, and each screen has a fixed personality that only hardens with experience.

As users mature, turn volume consolidates. The product page and the home screen absorb almost everything; every checkout-side screen shrinks.

One more thing the PDP data says clearly: people do not learn to ask Vaani earlier. A Vaani turn on a product page typically lands on the second product of that type the user has opened (median 2, mean 4–5). With experience that gets deeper, not shallower — from 4.22 same-type clicks before the turn in session one to 5.50 by session eleven. Vaani has become an end-of-crawl tool, used after the manual work is already done.

Insight — each surface already tells us what job it wants done there. We have not built to that; we have shipped one voice interface and let each screen figure out its own use.
06And the enormous stretch it is absent from

Before a shopper's first search, they look at 137 products in silence. Nothing intelligent happens in any of it.

We looked at 352,400 sessions and kept the 157,210 where someone browsed and then searched. The browsing before that search is not light. The mean is 137.1 products clicked through, the median 50, and 34.3% of these sessions see 100 or more.

Then they search — and 40.9% of the time the search is for a category they had not browsed at all in that session. Two thirds of the queries (65.9%) carry no attribute of any kind: "saree", "watch". Short, generic, unqualified.

Insight — there is a long, information-rich, completely silent stretch of every session where we know a great deal about what the shopper is doing and say nothing about it.

Implication

This is the single largest surface for the "show intelligence first" idea in Part II. A shopper who has clicked 50 products is telling us what they want; a one-word search is them failing to say it. That gap is exactly the not-expressing problem.

07Adoption: the ceiling

Almost nobody starts, and most of those who start never come back.

Across the pooled experiments, 87.4% of Test-arm users — 5.07M people who could have used Vaani — never touched it. In the zero-click experiment, which nudged harder than anything before it, 73.8% never used Vaani and only 3.4% ever had one real nudged conversation.

Of the people who do start, most stop immediately. 60–61% of adopters have exactly one Vaani session, ever. Only about 5% get past five.

Insight — this is not a retention problem with a leaky funnel. It is a first-contact problem: the product never demonstrates what it is, so there is nothing to come back for.
08The hardest push we could make

We nudged at the most precise moment we could find. Roughly one in eight people took it, and it wore out fast.

Zero-click nudges fire when a session is about to end with no product click at all — about as pointed as targeting gets. We ran it three ways across ~1.08M users per arm, and reached 21.4% (conservative) to 29.4% (aggressive) of the cohort.

The first thing we learned was about our own measurement. The original "adopted" flag meant a mic tap within 60 seconds. When we checked whether a real conversation followed, only 35.6% of those flagged adoptions had one. Roughly 61% of what we were calling adoption was a tap that went nowhere — a UI open, an error, an abandoned mic. Every number below uses the honest definition: tap and a real Vaani turn.

09What the uplift is actually worth

Vaani users order more. Most of that is because they were already better shoppers, and the part that is real is expensive.

The headline looks great: heavy Vaani users order about 4× as much as never-users. Then you control for how often someone opens the app — and most of the gap turns out to be app-open frequency, not Vaani. Restricting to users with at least 27 app opens shrinks the gap to about 1.4×. Stratifying into narrow app-open bands, so every cohort has a near-identical average, shrinks it further: the habit-formed bucket's real gap is +0.409 orders per user, down from +0.915 on the looser match.

And the thing we most wanted to be true is not: shopping is not getting easier for the people who use Vaani most.

There is one more counter-intuitive cut worth repeating. Split Vaani users by how much they lean on it per visit: the half that relies on Vaani most heavily orders less, despite spending 7× more turns. High reliance looks less like "loves Vaani" and more like "needed more help this visit".

Insight — today Vaani is a marker of a good shopper more than a cause of a better shopping journey. That is a fair thing to say about an assistive layer. It is not a good thing to say about an intelligence layer.
10Why this approach cannot get much further

There is one root cause with four faces. Every one of them is a property of the design, not of the model.

The root cause is simple to say: the user does not know what Vaani can do, and Vaani does nothing until they ask. A pull product cannot teach its own capabilities to someone who does not know what to pull. That is the paradox we have been trying to nudge our way out of.

Menus, not demos

A nudge names a category of question — "do you want to know?", "do you want this or that?". It tells the user what to say. It never shows them that Vaani is smart. Backed by the data: our best rule tops out at 13.6% adoption.

You miss it, you miss it

A voice nudge happens once and is gone. There is nothing to return to, nothing to scroll back to, no persistent record that Vaani exists. Measured: adoption drops from 8.0% to 3.2% by the seventh exposure of the same rule.

Voice alone cannot push

Three reasons: phone volume is low, households are crowded and shared, and TTS quality is weak. Two of the three are the user's environment, not our technology — which is the argument for delivering visually and using voice as reinforcement.

Not omnipresent

Vaani is a destination, not something woven into each screen's own job. Section 05 shows every surface already has a personality; we have not built anything native to any of them.

Everything we have built makes the app easier to operate. None of it makes a shopper discover what an intelligent assistant could do for them.

That is the honest summary of Part I. It is not a failure — the assistive layer works, converts, and is genuinely liked by the small group who found it. But it is a ceiling, and we can see the shape of it clearly enough now to say what should replace it.

Part II

What Vaani can become

The fix is not a better nudge. It is reversing the order of events: show the intelligence first, put our name on it, and let the user come to us. Then give that pull somewhere worth going.

11Flip the order

Stop selling the intelligence. Demonstrate it.

What we do today

Tell → ask the user to adopt → maybe demonstrate value.

An unknown thing asks the user to engage. The user has to imagine what it might be good for. Most don't, and we measured exactly how many: 87.4%.

What we should do

Show value → attribute it to Vaani → create curiosity → the user adopts.

Vaani does small useful things on its own, all over the app, and signs them. The user gets value before they are a user. Adoption becomes a consequence rather than a request.

Concretely, that means Vaani should independently do small intelligent things across the app and leave a lightweight signature behind: "Vaani found…", "Suggested by Vaani", "Vaani organised this for you", "Vaani thinks…". The intelligence comes first. The name comes attached to it. The invitation comes last.

The loop we are trying to start is the same one, run properly:

Written as a progression, this is the pattern every product in Section 16 followed:

Show

Do something genuinely useful without requiring adoption. No prompt, no invocation, no tutorial.

Attribute

Make it unmistakable that Vaani did it. Repeated association is what builds an entity in the user's head, rather than a scattering of smart features.

Interact

Let them react cheaply: yes, no, why, show me, more like this. Vaani has already produced the first turn; the user only has to answer.

Expand

Turn that reaction into a real Vaani conversation that already carries all the context. No blank box.

Delegate

Eventually the user stops operating the interface for that task and just says what they want done.

At any moment a shopper is in one of five states. Everything Vaani builds should map to one of them.

For the three middle states — Refine, Alternate, Decide — the user has exactly two problems, and we have exactly two moves:

Not knowing → show the path

They don't know the thing is possible. Filters exist, but they don't know they can refine; they don't know Vaani can pull alternatives; they don't know Vaani could help them decide. The fix is to show them, not tell them.

Not expressing → shorten the path

They know, but they can't do it. They can't pick the right filters, or say what "better" means, or phrase the ask. Section 06 is the proof: 66% of searches carry no attribute at all, and one-word searches continue prior browsing only 6.8% of the time.

13Secondary surfaces — the pull engine

Vaani as intelligence living inside every screen, doing that screen's own job. Not five miniature Vaani chats.

A secondary surface is not there to solve the whole problem. Its job is to produce evidence — evidence that Vaani sees, understands, remembers, can reason, can find things, can do work. For a non-adopter, this is effectively an AI mode built into Meesho: they benefit from Vaani before ever becoming a Vaani user.

Six things Vaani can do on a secondary surface:

A

Verdict

An observation that proves Vaani understands the situation. "20 products here are under ₹500." "Most of these are cotton." "You seem to be looking for red under ₹300."

A verdict is the cheapest possible conversation starter: the user only has to say "yes, show me those".

B

Suggestion

Products, substitutes, out-of-stock replacements, complements after a purchase — surfaced proactively. Some of this intelligence already exists elsewhere in Meesho; we can package it as a Vaani intervention so attribution accumulates to one entity.

The shift is not always a new model. Sometimes it is deciding who gets the credit.

C

Feedback & preference learning

Vaani shows its hypothesis and lets the user correct it. "You seem to be skipping green kurtis — show fewer?" "You opened this and came straight back. Didn't like it?"

Not a settings form. It should feel like being observed usefully.

D

Tip / nugget

"Pay prepaid and save ₹50." "Vaani recommends size M based on what you bought before." "Several buyers say this runs small."

Consumable in one glance, no conversation required. Voice can reinforce it, like a voice note.

E

Work already done

The strongest form. "While you were away, Vaani organised your saved products." "Vaani found a cheaper duplicate." "Vaani picked the best value among the four you shortlisted."

Far stronger than "would you like Vaani to organise this?" — do, then tell.

F

Functional Vaani

Track, cancel, return, exchange, refund, rate. Here intent already exists, so there is no pull to manufacture — make these Vaani-led by default.

Repetition teaches the habit: "when I need something done, I tell Vaani."

Three rules, and they are not negotiable:

Intelligence before invocation

Never open with "Ask Vaani…". Open with an actual useful output.

The intervention wears the screen's costume

Feed intelligence looks like a feed. PDP intelligence looks like a PDP. Vaani's identity travels between surfaces; Vaani's own UI does not.

Always leave a signature, and only when confident

If intelligence is happening anyway, decide deliberately when to sign it. But do not expose every inference — "you don't like cotton" needs real evidence and an easy correction.

14Primary surface — where outcomes actually improve

Don't touch browsing while browsing works. Once we know the direction, take real control.

Two principles. First: users already know how to browse, and the goal is not to make every interaction conversational. Let them browse until they reach an anchor product — something close enough that we now understand the direction of their intent. If it isn't broken, don't fix it.

Second: after the anchor, Vaani can become substantially more useful than answering questions. It can evaluate the product, compare it, find alternatives that hold what the user liked, change the one attribute they didn't, explain trade-offs, and help them decide.

There is a whole class of asks that today's UI simply cannot express:

"Show me dresses like this one, slightly cheaper, with longer sleeves."
"Which of these two is better on quality, price and delivery?"
"Is this actually a good product for me?"

Any intelligent solution that directly helps someone find or decide on the right product deserves a surface it owns. And that surface should not be a chat screen — it should be an interface generated for the mission in hand. For one user that is an A-versus-B comparison; for another, five visually similar alternatives; for another, a price-versus-quality trade-off; for another, a review synthesis plus a size explanation. The UI follows the decision being made.

Input

Voice first, text second. This is where Vaani's advantage is real — people say things they will never type, and Section 06 shows they type almost nothing.

Output

Whatever suits the job: product lists, comparisons, cards, infographics, generated layouts, text, voice. Conversational input does not require conversational output. For shopping, the best answer is usually visual.

That single distinction also resolves the third failure mode in Section 10: low volume, crowded households and weak TTS all stop mattering the moment the answer is something you can see.

The primary surface engages on high intent and high confidence — both, always. It is reserved for primary outcomes: recent missions, comparisons, alternatives, and intelligence about the feed the user is looking at right now.

It is also the most valuable learning instrument we could build, because it is a two-way window. We show users capabilities the current app cannot support; they show us what they expect an intelligent shopping product to do. Every session there is a user telling us the app wasn't enough. We already have a small, vivid version of this: the 40 heaviest Vaani users, who between them produced 29,317 turns.

15How the two halves connect

Secondary surfaces create belief. The primary surface converts belief into usage.

They are not two products. They are one loop:

A secondary surface spots an opportunity

The user is browsing; Vaani notices something worth saying and says it, in that screen's own language.

It shows enough to be worth a tap

Not the full solution — enough intelligence to create pull.

The primary surface takes over the hard part

Comparison, alternatives, trade-offs, decisions — with an interface built for that mission.

The interaction reveals real intent

What they want, what attributes matter, what they reject, what trade-offs they accept, what they finally choose.

That understanding feeds back

Into personalisation, into ranking, into new native app capabilities — and into better secondary interventions. Which drives more adoption.

The secondary interaction is Turn Zero. The user's tap is Turn One.

This is the part we must not get wrong. If someone taps "why does Vaani recommend this?", the primary surface cannot open blank. It should already know the screen, the product, the feed, the prior behaviour, the verdict it just gave, and the action the user took. Making the user restate context they just gave us is how a context-aware product stops feeling context-aware.

16Seven products that already did this

Nobody who succeeded at this started by asking users to visit the AI. They put the AI inside behaviour that already existed.

Google Search

AI Overview → AI Mode → one continuous thing

Users kept searching normally. For queries where a generative answer helped, an AI Overview appeared above the results — no mode to enter, no prompting to learn. Google later found users were trying to trigger more Overviews and asking longer questions. Only then did AI Mode ship, driven by users who wanted more. By January 2026 you could ask a follow-up straight from an Overview and flow into AI Mode carrying the original search context.

This is the exact shape we want: feed verdict → "show me" → primary surface opens already knowing the category, price band, fabric and the products on screen.

Notion said teams saw significantly more value after that change alone. It then moved into ambient work: automatic meeting notes, search that answers instead of linking, an agent that does multi-step work.

The pattern

The same six stages, every time

0 — algorithms exist but there is no intelligent entity in the user's mind. 1 — visible micro-outcomes appear with no behaviour change required. 2 — they get attributed to one named thing. 3 — lightweight reaction becomes possible. 4 — context carries into a real conversation. 5 — heavy users get a primary destination. 6 — the AI starts shaping how the product itself is presented.

We are stuck between stage 0 and stage 5, having skipped 1 through 4. That is the actual diagnosis of our adoption problem.

17Where this ends

Over time, the line between the app, the secondary surface and Vaani should stop being visible.

Today the app is fixed and AI sits on top of it. In the middle state the app and Vaani have separate surfaces and hand users between them. Eventually the interface itself becomes modular and responsive to intent — at which point a fixed app made of fixed screens is increasingly beside the point.

Phased, so it reads as a plan and not a manifesto:

Vaani starts leaving evidence

Small intelligent things across feed, PDP, wishlist, cart, orders and payments. No conversation required anywhere.

Every piece of intelligence becomes interactive

"Show more", "correct that", "why?", "show me". Secondary surfaces become conversation starters written by Vaani instead of prompts invented by the user.

Secondary becomes Turn Zero of primary

Context carries. The primary surface never opens blank.

Vaani expands for users who show pull

Prominence should not be identical for everyone. Users who repeatedly expand verdicts, accept refinements and ask follow-ups have told us AI is becoming their preferred control mechanism — give them more of it. This is our version of the AI Overview to AI Mode graduation.

Vaani becomes the way the app is operated

The user states an outcome; Vaani orchestrates the product to get there. Traditional UX doesn't disappear — it becomes evidence, control, confirmation and visual representation of what Vaani is doing.

Which is why this work should not be described as an adoption hack. Adding entry points is the cheap reading. What secondary surfaces actually are is the migration mechanism from an app-first interaction model to an intelligence-first one — and that migration can only be taught through demonstrated usefulness, one small proof at a time.

18The guardrail

Omnipresent is not the same as inescapable.

Instagram shows the failure mode as well as the pattern. Users pushed back when Meta AI felt imposed — AI answers where people wanted accounts. The line between omnipresent and inescapable is whether the intervention serves the screen's intent or hijacks it. Our framing already contains the safeguard — solve each screen's own job — but it should be an explicit principle: Vaani appears where it makes the screen's job easier, never where it interrupts it.

This matters more for us than for Instagram. Our users have lower digital confidence than Instagram's median. A confused first-timer who feels ambushed does not complain. They just avoid. And Section 10 already showed what avoidance looks like at scale.

So we need a real measure for it, not a good intention. What tells us a Vaani touchpoint is interrupting rather than serving? That is an open question and the first thing that will erode under growth pressure.

Part III

What we are testing next

One narrow build, chosen because it exercises the whole argument at once and because the data screams loudest there. If it works we have conviction. If it doesn't we learn something specific rather than something vague.

19Why price turns are the place to start

"Is this the best price?" is not a discount request. It is the shopper asking whether to buy this one.

Everything lines up here. Price is roughly 70% of DECIDE turns on the product page at every browse depth. 44.6% of price turns are outright negotiation. It is our largest ask, our worst-answered ask, and the exact moment a shopper is deciding.

The reason is visible in the same data. When Vaani does search after a decision turn, it almost never carries forward the thing the user was just talking about:

Underneath the ask sits well-established behaviour. Buyers anchor on one product and then grind through 7.1 more clicks of the same product type before ordering; 75% of post-anchor clicks stay inside the same category tree. They are not chasing the price floor — the alternative they click before buying is priced above the eventual purchase about as often as below — but any gap multiplies the work: anchoring at twice the price they finally pay costs +56% more browsing. And the strongest purchase signal on the whole app is a shopper re-opening a product to compare it:

So

The shopper asking about price is asking for an alternative-discovery and decision answer. We have never given them one. That is the POC.

20What we are building

On a price turn: search visually similar products with a price filter, rerank them so the good ones land first, then say out loud which is the better buy and why.

Three scopes, deliberately separable so each one's contribution is measurable on its own.

Scope 1 — backend only

Put the answer where the thumb already is

  • Detect price intent on a PDP Vaani turn and fire an anchored image search using the product's own catalog image.
  • Apply the adjacent price filter and an availability constraint. Take the top 50.
  • Rerank deterministically on six P0 fields — price, rating, rating count, seller rating, availability, Mall/Gold — with config-driven weights. No LLM anywhere in the ranking path.
  • Badge Mall and Gold on the card, and lift them in the rerank.
Scope 2 — the verdict

The first real comparison capability in the product

  • Assemble a payload: the anchor with its selected variant, plus the top 6–7 reranked products, each carrying its rank and its P0/P1/P2 fields.
  • Vaani speaks a verdict comparing the anchor against those results — which is the better buy, and why.
  • Honour the size or variant already chosen on the anchor, so we never recommend something the buyer cannot wear.
  • A silence path: when nothing is genuinely better, say so. Filler costs us the next conversation — Section 04 quantifies exactly how much.
  • Validators after generation for unsupported claims and positional mismatch.
Scope 3 — the surface

The first genuine primary surface

  • A server-driven custom listing page that opens on a price turn, with backend-composed content slots.
  • The 50-candidate pool grouped by an LLM against a fixed six-group taxonomy — so we learn which axes buyers actually shop by.
  • The anchor product pinned, for comparison.
  • This is Section 14 in miniature: an interface generated for the decision being made, not a chat window.

Deliberately cut, not deferred. No Vaani-driven navigation — no scroll-to, no deep-linking, no opening a curated product list. Reranking is the substitute: put the answer at the top instead of walking the buyer to it. No price negotiation of any kind — Vaani never changes a price, promises a discount, or implies one exists. No LLM in ranking; the model explains the order, it never produces it. Carry-over of earlier session filters is deferred past Scope 3; only the price filter and the anchor's own selected variant travel.

21What would prove us right, or wrong

Four claims, each one falsifiable, each with the metric that settles it.

A price ask is an alternative-discovery intent, not a discount request

If true, a reranked alternative feed raises same-category alternate purchase rate against today's price answer. If false, buyers abandon the feed and re-ask for a discount.

Deterministic reranking on six fields beats default order

Measured by top-3 product open rate — the direct read on whether we put the answer where the thumb is. Testable with no LLM in the path at all.

A spoken verdict converts better than a silent reorder

Scope 1 changes what the buyer sees; Scope 2 changes what they understand. The delta between them is the value of the verdict, and it is measurable as an isolated increment.

Buyers can act on a voice reference without a deep link

Measured by post-verdict open rate. If it is weak, the navigation we cut has to come back — and we will know that rather than guess it.

The effort metric ties straight back to Part I: clicks to decision after the turn, against the 7.1 post-anchor baseline. A working alternative engine cuts it. Section 09 is the reason this matters — effort has been rising, and this is the first build aimed squarely at making it fall.

Explicitly not the primary metric: within-Vaani order rate. That is selection, not lift — buyers who reach Vaani late in the hunt already convert better. We judge this on effort and same-type conversion.

22Why this one build

It is the smallest thing that tests the entire thesis at once.

It starts as a secondary intervention: on the product page, in the product page's own language, on a turn the user already initiated — Vaani doing something useful and putting its name on it. It graduates into a primary surface: a generated listing page built for the comparison the buyer is actually making. It carries context across that boundary, so the surface is Turn Zero and the tap is Turn One. And it delivers the one capability our data says shoppers need most and ask for least.

It also gives us the first honest answer to the question the whole of Part II rests on:

Does demonstrated intelligence create pull where a menu of questions did not?

If yes, the rest of the surface map in Section 13 becomes a roadmap rather than a proposal, and we start working it screen by screen. If no, we will know it at the level of a specific mechanism — the verdict, the rerank, the handoff — rather than concluding vaguely that "adoption is hard".

Open decisions

Four things we still have to settle

These are not blockers for the POC. They are the decisions that will shape everything after it, and they are better made deliberately than by default.

  1. Which screen gets the first full secondary integration? The mission-state map suggests feed for Start and Refine, PDP for Alternate and Decide, orders and history for Resume. Sequencing is undecided.
  2. What is Vaani's identity thread? The persistent visual mark that ties every scattered touchpoint back to one entity — our equivalent of Meta's gradient ring. Without it we get lots of smart features and no smart assistant.
  3. Nudge placement per screen. Each screen's outcome nudge should map to that screen's own job. Nudge placement and omnipresence are the same workstream under two names — we should stop planning them separately.
  4. How do we measure imposition? What tells us a Vaani touchpoint is interrupting rather than serving? This is the first thing that erodes under growth pressure, so it needs a number before it needs defending.

How to read this document

Part I is deliberately unsparing. Several numbers here are worse than earlier versions of the same analysis, because the analyses were corrected rather than defended — the nudge adoption rates roughly halved when we required a real conversation rather than a mic tap, and the order uplift shrank by more than half when we matched properly on app-open frequency. Those corrections are the reason to trust the rest.

Most of Part I is correlational. Users self-select into Vaani engagement, so some of what looks like a Vaani effect reflects who chooses to engage. Where a selection story is the more likely explanation — the re-search conversion result in Section 19 is the clearest case — it is stated rather than glossed over. The conclusions in Part II do not depend on the causal reading; they depend on the volumes, the concentrations and the absences, which are not in doubt.

Part II is a direction, not a spec. The surface map in Section 13 is a menu of opportunities, ordered by evidence, not a commitment to build all of it. Part III is the only thing here with a date attached.
