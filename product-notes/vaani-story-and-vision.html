Vaani · The Future of the Product · August 2026

Show first, ask later

How Vaani earns adoption by demonstrating intelligence everywhere — and where that takes the Meesho experience.

2.20MVaani turns analysed, 167,082 users
58.6%FIND — show me something
29.9%DECIDE — help me decide
6.5%of adopters reach 5+ sessions
87.4%of exposed users never adopt
The paradox: we built a pull product for users who don't know what to pull. Vaani can only teach its capabilities to someone who already knows what to ask. Our first-time buyer doesn't — and never will, unless the product makes the first move.
00Our thoughts in a snapshot

Two clear problems, and both are a consequence of how we chose to build the product.

Vaani works. It just works for very few people, and for a narrow slice of what shopping actually requires. Everything in this document sits under one of two headings.

Problem one

Adoption

87.4% of users who could use Vaani never speak to it once. Of those who do, 64.5% never come back for a second session. Our sharpest nudge converts 12.9–14.1%, and decays every time it fires.

→ Fixed by secondary surfaces (§06–08)

Problem two

Outcomes, when the user is deciding

When Vaani fetches, it works — 74.4% good outcomes. When Vaani has to reason out loud, it does not: 28.4% at best, and 12.6% when it asks a question back. Deciding and finding alternatives is where this bites.

→ Fixed by a primary surface (§09–11)

Both problems have the same root. We built Vaani as a voice layer on top of an app that was already finished. It can make the existing app easier to operate. It cannot do things the app was never designed to do — and it cannot teach anyone what it is capable of.
01How Vaani was built

Useful, but capped by the app it serves.

The first version was deliberately narrow: voice-first, very limited UX intervention, intelligence used to make existing journeys easier. That was the right way to start — it let us learn how users behave with conversational help before rebuilding shopping around it.

Each of those is genuinely valuable, and voice removes real friction — spelling, taxonomy, the effort of translating a need into search terms. But notice what they have in common: every one of them operates a surface the app already had.

The app remains the experience; Vaani just helps operate it. The missing piece is a surface Vaani owns.

One thing we did not plan for: a new modality surfaced demand that could never have appeared in the old construct. People started negotiating on price, at volume, because speaking made it possible. That is the first evidence that new ways to engage produce new opportunities — and it is the seed of everything in the second half of this document.

Problem one · Adoption

02How many adopt, and how many reach habit

Habit is real. Almost nobody gets there.

The good news sits at the far end of that funnel. Once a user reaches five sessions, the behaviour becomes self-sustaining: 68% continue to another session, 89.6% self-initiate rather than waiting to be nudged, and 64.6% use Vaani again within their next five app opens.

Habit formation is not the problem. Reaching it is. Whoever forms a habit sticks with Vaani. The entire loss is between session one and session two.

Problem one · Adoption

03Why adoption is low

The reason is the approach we took, not the targeting.

Today's nudges are menus, not demos. "Do you want to know?" tells the user what to say — not why Vaani is smart. The user has to imagine the value before ever seeing it. And it is spoken once, then gone.

Low volume

Phones stay muted or quiet. Voice alone cannot be relied on to carry the nudge.

Dense households

Shared, noisy spaces make audio easy to lose and socially awkward to use.

Weak TTS, one shot

Output quality undermines trust, and there is no second chance to hear it.

Two of those three are the user's environment, not our technology — which points squarely at visual-first delivery.

We tested this as hard as it can be tested

Zero-click nudges fire when a session looks highly likely to end without a single product click — about as conservative and well-targeted as a trigger gets. Even there, adoption caps out.

Vaani was effective for adopters, and irrelevant to everyone who ignored it. When a nudge does produce a conversation, the session recovers — zero-click sessions fall from 32.6% to 12.8%. The mechanism works. Almost nobody enters it.

Problem one · Adoption

04What habit-formed users show us

The people who get through do better — and it costs us to get them there.

This matters because it tells us the product is worth adopting. The barrier is discovery, not value.

Density beats channel

Deep users had 16.7 vs 5.9 searches and 9.1 vs 2.4 turns in their first session. Search-led first sessions bounced at 41.7% vs 60.2%.

Channel barely matters

Nudge-first and organic-first users form habit at 7.0% vs 6.2%. Acquisition was never the difference. What happened after adoption was.

But repeat can be friction

Price is highly repetitive — 8.7 turns per user — yet only 29.6% successful. Repetition without resolution is an unanswered question, not a habit.

Vaani users order more — but the effort is going up, not down

We are creating impact, for a very small number of users, at a high cost per outcome. Both halves of that sentence point the same way: the constraint is not how much Vaani talks. It is what Vaani is able to do when it does.

Problem two · Outcomes

05How Vaani is actually used, and where it breaks

Vaani is already two products: "show me" and "help me decide." It is good at one of them.

What counts as a good outcome. For FIND, the user clicks a product Vaani showed them. For DECIDE, the user adds to cart after asking Vaani something. On that definition, here is what works.

That ordering is the whole diagnosis of problem two. Do the thing > answer specifically > answer generically > ask the user to try again. Our largest ask by volume, price, is one we can only answer with words — so it lands at 29.6%.

And this is what starts happening over sessions

The deciding journey is where the money is — and where we are weakest

Alternate is not "more like this." It is: preserve what the user likes, change what they do not. That is a state-management problem before it is a retrieval problem — and the current app has nowhere to hold that state.
06Why both problems have one shape

Trust is the hinge, and two frictions block every capability.

Everything above resolves into one loop. Vaani only earns a second use if the outcome was trustworthy — so the loop's first node and its third node have to carry the same currency: outcomes.

Today the loop breaks at node one: the nudge is a menu, so it never produces the outcome that would earn node four.

Underneath, every capability is blocked by one of two frictions

Not knowing — our job: show the path

They can't want what they can't see

The user doesn't know a capability exists, so they never reach for it.

e.g. The feed can be narrowed with filters — but the user has never discovered that filters exist.

→ Surface the capability at the exact moment it becomes useful.

Not expressing — our job: shorten the path

They know, but can't operate it

The user knows the capability exists, but can't work it efficiently enough to reach their goal.

e.g. They know filters exist — but can't combine the right ones to land on what they have in mind.

→ Deliver the outcome without demanding mastery of the mechanics.

We can measure both. Not knowing is the 87.4% who never adopt, and the 556 comparison asks. Not expressing is visible in every search box: two-thirds of queries carry no attribute at all, and a one-word query continues the shopper's own prior browsing only 6.8% of the time.

Intelligence is the first tool we have that can solve both frictions at once — show the path where the user doesn't know, and shorten the path where they can't express. The next two sections are those two jobs, given two surfaces.

The fix

07Two surfaces, two jobs

Secondary gets users to intelligence. Primary puts it to work.

One surface fixes adoption, the other fixes outcomes. They are not two products — they are a division of responsibility. If the existing surface can complete the task well, Vaani should assist it. If the task needs reasoning or an interface the app does not have, Vaani should own that part of the journey.

01 · Fixes adoption

Outcome-based nudges, in UI and voice

Nudges show actual outcomes Vaani produced — not capability categories. UI persistence fixes every voice failure at once: volume, privacy, TTS quality, transience.

The nudge becomes the demo — a nudge, a proof of intelligence and a trust deposit in one.

02 · Fixes outcomes

Vaani woven into every real estate

A secondary integration inside each surface, solving that surface's own intent. It educates users about Vaani as a side effect of usefulness, never a tutorial.

Seeds trust so users arrive at the primary surface already convinced.

Fixing adoption

08Secondary surfaces

Six ways to show intelligence before asking for anything.

A secondary surface is not another Vaani button. It is Vaani doing a small, useful thing inside the task the user is already doing — and signing it.

Verdict

"20 products here are under ₹500." Shows Vaani understands what you are looking at.

Suggestion

"Suggested by Vaani" — a cheaper substitute, a complement, an in-stock alternative.

Preference learning

"You seem to skip green kurtis — show fewer?" A hypothesis the user can correct.

Tip / nugget

"Pay prepaid, save ₹50." "Buyers say this runs small." Consumable without a conversation.

Work already done

"While you were away, Vaani organised your wishlist." Doing beats asking to do.

Functional Vaani

Track, cancel, return, exchange, review — Vaani-led flows where intent already exists.

Some of this intelligence already exists inside Meesho. Packaging it as Vaani is what builds one consistent, attributable identity.

Fixing outcomes

10The primary surface

Don't fix browsing. Own what comes after.

Users already know how to browse. The goal is not "make every interaction conversational." Let them browse naturally until intelligence can materially improve the journey — then take real control.

Evaluate · Compare · Alternate · Decide

"Browse → find anchor" becomes "anchor → understand intent → Vaani actively completes the mission."

The primary surface is not a chat screen. It is a Vaani-owned shopping surface for the missions the app cannot express — "same look but cheaper", "which of these should I buy?", "continue what I was looking at yesterday". The principle is task first, UI second: understand the mission, then render the interface that solves it.

Voice in, anything out. Input stays conversational — voice primary, text secondary — because it lets users express complex intent cheaply. Output is whatever the decision needs: comparisons, alternatives, infographics, generated UI. Conversational input does not require conversational output.

Meesho → user

Show them the future

Expose capabilities far richer than the current app supports: personalised comparisons, anchor-preserving alternatives, review synthesis, size visualisation — UI generated per mission.

User → Meesho

Let them tell us what it should be

An unconstrained interface reveals what users actually expect: what to automate, where the app fails, which decisions they struggle with. A discovery mechanism for the future product.

We use the primary surface to show users the future of Meesho — while users use it to tell us what that future should be.

The first experiment

12What we are testing now

Alternate product discovery and comparison, on a Vaani surface.

We are not building the vision at once. The first experiment takes the sharpest instance of problem two — the price turn — and gives it a surface that can actually hold the mission.

Bet 1 · Alternate product discovery

Anchor the product, capture the dissatisfaction, preserve must-keep attributes, change the failing constraint, show 5–10 alternatives with reasons, and keep return to the anchor trivial.

Bet 2 · Comparison

Compare products the shopper actually considered, on price, reviews, seller, delivery and fit. Explain the trade-off rather than manufacturing a winner. The largest unserved job we have.

Bet 3 · Secondary pull

Two or three high-confidence interventions on PDP, feed and wishlist. Show intelligence before asking for adoption, and measure interruption as carefully as pull.

Bet 4 · Turn-0 handoff

Carry surface, product, verdict and behaviour into primary. The user should never repeat what Vaani already knew.

Step four is not hypothetical. Across the 40 heaviest Vaani users and the 29,317 turns between them, voice corrected, contradicted or wholly supplied what the platform believed for 26 of the 40 — and 8 are invisible in our order systems entirely. The same conversations surfaced 245 unmet asks, 163 explicit rejections and 144 stated price ceilings, none of which exist in click data.

The migration path — five phases from feature to interface

1

Evidence

Vaani leaves visible proof of intelligence across feed, PDP, cart, wishlist and orders. No conversation required anywhere.

2

Interactive

Every intervention can be acted on — "yes, show more", "why?", "no, correct that". Vaani starts the conversation instead of waiting for it.

3

Turn Zero

Tapping an intervention opens the primary surface pre-loaded with surface, product and behaviour. The tap is Turn 1.

4

Expand

Users who show pull get more: richer outputs, more prominence, less friction into primary Vaani. Prominence follows demonstrated demand.

5

Vaani is the interface

Users tell Vaani the outcome; Meesho becomes the environment through which Vaani accomplishes it.

Not a transition we force in one release — we teach the interaction model through demonstrated utility.

This is not a chatbot replacing the app. Visual browsing stays valuable. The shift is that users no longer have to operate the interface manually to get an outcome. Over time the question "is this Vaani or Meesho?" should stop mattering.

Six beliefs the surfaces have to build

"It understands what I'm doing"
"It notices things useful to me"
"It does work before I even ask"
"It remembers my journey"
"I can correct it when it's wrong"
"For anything bigger, I can just ask it"
Vaani becomes the intelligence layer through which Meesho adapts itself around what each user is trying to accomplish. Secondary surfaces create belief. The primary surface converts belief into usage.
14Proof from the industry

Everyone who cracked AI adoption cracked it the same way.

Google Searchsource ↗

AI Overviews appeared inside normal search. Users began asking longer questions and wanting more — then AI Mode carried the context forward.

LessonSecondary to primary handoff, with context. Exactly §11.

Gmailsource ↗

12% of mobile replies came from Smart Reply by 2017. Three suggested words grew, over years, into an inbox assistant.

LessonNo step required a new behaviour. Each one made the next smaller.

Amazonsource ↗

Shopping Guides, review highlights and "Help Me Decide" show intelligence before any chat. Help Me Decide is triggered by browsing without buying — behaviour, not a question.

LessonRufus is the follow-up, not the front door. Our revisit pattern is the same trigger.

X / Groksource ↗

Moved from a nav-bar destination to a button on the post itself. The object becomes the prompt.

LessonThe surface carries the context; the user never rebuilds it.

Meta AIsource ↗

Dissolved into search, DMs, feed and creation — each in that surface's own idiom. The standalone app came only after the identity was learned.

LessonOne identity, many local costumes. The destination comes last.

Notion & Wordsource ↗

Both removed the empty prompt box: contextual one-click suggestions, and automatic document summaries with chat one click away.

LessonValue with zero prompting. "Not knowing what to ask" solved by showing the path.

One caution, from the same examples. Users pushed back when Meta AI felt imposed. The line between omnipresent and inescapable is whether the integration serves the surface's intent — or hijacks it.
