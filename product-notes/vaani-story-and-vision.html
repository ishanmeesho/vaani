Vaani · Product Future State · August 2026

From a voice feature to an intelligent shopping layer

What we built, how people actually use Vaani, why first-time adoption has a ceiling, and what the product should become next.

2.20MVaani turns analysed, 167,082 users
58.6%FIND — show me something
29.9%DECIDE — help me decide
6.5%of adopters reach 5+ sessions
87.4%of exposed users never adopt
Vaani 1.0 proved that intelligence can make Meesho easier to use. The next chapter is to make intelligence itself a way to shop.
01The argument

Users are already pulling Vaani into real shopping jobs. The product still makes them discover the AI before they can see its value.

Users took Vaani further than we designed for

They do not treat it as a chatbot. 58.6% of turns are FIND and 29.9% are DECIDE — the job moves from "show me" to "help me decide." Price alone is 19% of everything said to Vaani. The demand for decision help is already here.

The ceiling is architecture, not adoption

Even at full adoption, today's Vaani is bounded by what the existing app can already show or do. Our heaviest users are not shopping with less effort — they click more per order, not fewer. That is a design limit, not a usage limit.

Show intelligence, and adoption follows

Secondary surfaces make useful intelligence visible inside Meesho before the user asks. Primary surfaces take over when the mission needs reasoning the app cannot express. Over time the boundary between them disappears.

The job changes by surface. Home and search are mission launch and refinement; the product page is a decision desk around price, quality, fit and pack.

Habit is real but narrow. 64.5% of adopters are one-and-done; 6.5% reach five sessions or more. Once they do, usage becomes self-initiated.

A good first outcome beats repeated prompting. Price repeats after a good first answer 84.0% of the time, against 62.8% after a bad one.

Pointed nudges found a real need moment, not a discovery model. Strict adoption is 12.9–14.1%, and it decays with every repeat.

The shift: from "Vaani is something users invoke" to "Vaani understands the shopping mission and makes relevant intelligence continuously discoverable."

03Where adoption stalls

Habit is real. The fight is session one to session two.

Adopted — at least one Vaani session97,646
Repeat — two or more33,918 · 34.7%
Engaged — three or more16,869 · 17.3%
Habit — five or more6,361 · 6.5%

Once a user reaches five sessions the behaviour is self-sustaining: 68% continue to another session, 89.6% self-initiate, and 64.6% use Vaani within their next five app opens. Habit is not the problem. Reaching it is — and behind this funnel sits the wider number: 87.4% of users who could use Vaani never speak to it once.

What earns the second session is the quality of the first answer

Density beats channel

Deep users had 16.7 vs 5.9 searches and 9.1 vs 2.4 turns in their first session. Search-led first sessions bounced at 41.7% vs 60.2%.

Channel barely matters

Nudge-first and organic-first users form habit at similar rates — 7.0% vs 6.2%. The difference was never acquisition. It was what happened after.

Repeat can be friction

Price is highly repetitive — 8.7 turns per user — but only 29.6% successful. Repetition without resolution is an unanswered question, not a habit.

Our sharpest nudge found the moment, but not a way to teach the product

We triggered Vaani when a session looked likely to end without a product click — about as pointed as targeting gets. When it lands, it works: the share of those sessions ending with no click falls from 32.6% to 12.8%, and 52.8% of adopted turns click Vaani's own result. But only 12.9–14.1% of users shown the nudge take it, roughly 61% of mic taps never become a real conversation, and about 1 in 5 of those who do adopt return on their own.

Menus, not demos

Nudges tell users a category of question they could ask. They almost never show the intelligence itself.

You miss it, you miss it

A transient voice nudge makes capability discovery depend on the exact moment. If it disappears, so does the lesson.

Voice is weak for push

Low volume, shared households and TTS quality make voice a poor medium for proactive discovery. It stays excellent once intent exists.

Vaani is not omnipresent

It is not woven into each surface's objective, so users see a separate feature rather than intelligence inside the shopping experience.

The first-time problem is structural: users have low comprehension of what Vaani can do, and Vaani is pull-based until the user already knows what to ask.
04The bigger limit

Suppose adoption were solved tomorrow. Today's Vaani would still be bounded by the app we already built.

Direct action on FIND is genuinely strong. But the current product can only express what existing Meesho constructs already support — if the answer is not already a feed, a product module, a search result or an app action, Vaani has nowhere to put it.

The question changes from "How do we get more users to invoke Vaani?" to "Where should intelligence show up, and when should it own the experience?"
05The model · two surfaces, two jobs

Vaani needs two surfaces because it has two different jobs.

The rule is simple. If the existing surface can complete the task well, Vaani should assist it. If the task needs reasoning or an interface the app does not have, Vaani should own that part of the journey.

  • Make one useful outcome visible
  • Attribute it clearly to Vaani
  • Keep the interaction lightweight
  • Become Turn 0 of primary when the user wants more

06Secondary surfaces

Show value before asking for adoption.

Today the first interaction asks users to trust Vaani before Vaani has demonstrated anything. Secondary surfaces reverse the sequence: observe context → do something useful → attribute it to Vaani → let the user react → expand only if they want more.

There is a long, silent stretch of every session where we already know what the shopper wants

Before a first search, shoppers click through a mean of 137 products. Then two-thirds of them search with no attribute at all — "saree", "watch" — and 41% search a category they never browsed that session. They know what they want. They cannot say it.

Proposed

What Vaani starts doing across the app

Deliberately small, and always solving the local job first: a feed verdict, a review summary, a better-priced substitute, a size recommendation, a preference check, an order action.

Feed & search results

"Vaani found 14 cotton kurtis under ₹500 in these results."
"You have skipped most synthetic fabrics — avoid them?"

Actions stay light: Show only these · Show more · Not really.

Product page

"Vaani's take: buyers like the fabric, but sizing is inconsistent."
"Vaani recommends size M based on your past purchases."
"Delivery is slower than the other option you viewed."

Actions: Why? · Compare · Show alternatives.

Wishlist & recently viewed

"Vaani found a cheaper option similar to this."
"These two are close substitutes — compare?"
"This is out of stock; I found three close replacements."

Cart & checkout

High-confidence only: "Vaani found a similar product ₹80 cheaper."
"Pay prepaid and save ₹50."

Home & search entry

Make ranking intelligence visible, and shorten the path rather than restarting intent.
"Continue with cotton kurtis under ₹500?"

Orders & post-purchase

"Vaani found matching leggings for what you bought."
For functional jobs be direct: track, cancel, return, exchange, refund. Intent already exists; Vaani is the shortest path.

Omnipresent does not mean inescapable. If an intervention does not make the current surface easier or more useful, it should not be there.
07Primary surface

Give Vaani room to own the outcome, not just answer inside the current app construct.

The primary surface is not a chat screen. It is a Vaani-owned shopping surface for missions that need reasoning across products, signals or time — "same look but cheaper", "which of these should I buy?", "continue what I was looking at yesterday".

The behaviour that justifies it

A broad re-search is the wrong answer to it

Alternate is not "more like this." It is: preserve what the user likes, change what they do not. That is a state-management problem before it is a retrieval problem.
Proposed

What belongs on the primary surface

The principle is task first, UI second: understand the mission, then render the interface that solves it. Voice stays the input because it lets users express complex intent cheaply — but the output should be visual whenever visual is clearer. Voice-first should never become voice-only.

MissionWhat Vaani ownsWhat the interface needs
AlternatePreserve what the user likes; change the failing constraint.Pinned anchor, must-keep / must-change, 5–10 alternatives with reasons, easy return
CompareOrganise real candidates on the dimensions that matter here.Side-by-side evidence, user-specific criteria, explicit trade-offs
DecideSynthesize price, quality, reviews, seller, delivery and fit.Verdict, evidence, follow-up actions
ResumeCarry a past mission forward instead of restarting.Previous anchors, expressed preferences, what changed
08How the two connect

The secondary surface is Turn 0. The user's tap is Turn 1.

A user should never restate context Vaani already had on screen. If Vaani says on a product page "buyers like the fabric but sizing is inconsistent," and the user taps compare with the other one, primary should open already knowing the screen, the product, the previous product, the verdict and the action that triggered it.

Two triggers, one architecture

The first is entered through an intent the user already had; the second through a behaviour they did not know could be read. Both put intelligence in the surface's own idiom.

The entry point is the intent, not the brand

Nobody taps "Price history" because they want to use an AI. They tap it because they want to know if ₹3,148 is a good price. Adoption is a by-product of answering the question they already had.

It creates the ask we never get

Comparison is asked of Vaani 556 times in 2.2M because nothing suggests it is possible. A strip of what you have been looking at, with one button above it, is how that demand becomes visible.

The same handoff, on our surfacesSecondary · Turn 0Primary continuation
Feed → Refine"Vaani found 14 cotton kurtis under ₹500.""Show me" opens a curated result already carrying cotton, price band and feed context

The connection works in reverse too. Once Vaani has generated alternatives or completed a comparison, the user moves back into normal browsing — images, variants, cart, checkout. Each surface owns the part of the journey it is best at.

10Where this ends

The transition matters as much as the end-state.

We do not jump from today's Vaani to "Vaani becomes the app." App-first and intelligence-first journeys coexist, and Vaani's prominence grows only where users demonstrate pull.

11What we are doing next

Prove the architecture with a small number of high-intent, measurable bets.

We are not building the vision at once. The next phase proves three things: a Vaani-owned primary surface creates a better outcome than the current app construct; secondary surfaces create pull without interrupting the journey; and context passes between them without a reset.

Bet 1 · Alternate product discovery

Anchor a product, capture the dissatisfaction, preserve must-keep attributes, change the failing constraint, show 5–10 alternatives with reasons, and keep return to the anchor trivial.

Bet 2 · Comparison

Compare products the shopper actually considered, on price, reviews, seller, delivery and fit. Explain the trade-off rather than manufacturing a winner. The largest unserved job we have.

Bet 3 · Secondary pull

Two or three high-confidence interventions on the product page, feed and wishlist. Show intelligence before asking for adoption, and measure interruption as carefully as pull.

Bet 4 · Turn-0 handoff

Carry surface, product, verdict and behaviour into primary. The user should never repeat what Vaani already knew.

The measure is not Vaani turns. It is whether Vaani improved the mission, earned trust, and created later voluntary use.
