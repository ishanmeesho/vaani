Part I · What we built, and where it stopped

01How Vaani was built

Useful, but capped by the app it serves.

The first version was deliberately narrow: voice-first, very limited UX intervention, intelligence used to make existing journeys easier. That was the right way to start — it let us learn how users behave with conversational help before rebuilding shopping around it.

Because Vaani lived inside the app as one feature rather than being the thing the app was built around, every outcome it could serve had to land on a screen that already existed. So we pointed it at problems we already understood: usability gaps and funnel drop-offs. Three capabilities came out of that framing.

⚲

Initiate search

Helps the user start looking for a product, in their own words rather than the app's taxonomy.

☰

Refine the feed

Narrows down what is being shown, without the user having to find and combine filters.

?

Understand a product

Answers questions about a product already on the app — price, quality, size, delivery.

Alongside them we built an adoption machine: a floating mic button, page-intro nudges on the product page, listing page and cart, and later zero-click nudges that fire when a session is about to end with nothing clicked.

Each capability is genuinely valuable, and voice removes real friction — spelling, taxonomy, the effort of turning a need into search terms. One user put it plainly:

"Spelling nahi aati hai toh … bol karke phir aa jaati hai."

But notice what those three capabilities have in common: every one of them operates a surface the app already had. Vaani could operate those surfaces better. It could rarely create a new experience around the user's mission.

The app remains the experience; Vaani just helps operate it. The missing piece is a surface Vaani owns.

One thing we did not plan for. A new modality surfaced demand that could never have appeared in the old construct: people started negotiating on price, at volume, because speaking made it possible in a way that tapping never did. Nobody designed that. It arrived because we changed how users could express themselves, and it is now the single largest thing anyone says to Vaani.

The signal worth holding onto — new ways to engage produce new demand. That is the argument for the second half of this document in miniature.

Part I · What we built, and where it stopped

02The first ceiling — adoption

Almost nobody starts, and the ones who start rarely come back.

Two months of data, one funnel. Of everyone who could have used Vaani, the overwhelming majority never speak to it once — and of those who do, most never return for a second session.

Read down that funnel and the loss is concentrated in one place. It is not a leaky funnel with attrition spread across many steps; it is a wall between the first session and the second. 34.7% of adopters reach a second session, 17.3% a third. Everything after that decays gently rather than sharply. Which tells us the problem is not retention mechanics. It is the first experience, and what precedes it.

We tested the entry problem as hard as it can be tested

Zero-click nudges fire when a session looks highly likely to end without a single product click — a moment of obvious, measurable need, and about as conservative and well-targeted as a trigger gets. If teaching people the capability through nudging were going to work anywhere, it would work here.

The first thing we learned was about our own measurement. The original "adopted" flag meant a mic tap within 60 seconds. When we checked whether a real conversation actually followed, only 35.6% of those flagged adoptions had one. Roughly 61% of what we were calling adoption was a tap that went nowhere — a UI open, an error, an abandoned mic. Every number below uses the honest definition: a tap and a real Vaani turn.

None of which means nudges are worthless. When a zero-click nudge actually produces a conversation, the effect on that session is large and immediate.

Vaani was effective for adopters, and irrelevant to everyone who ignored it. The mechanism works. Almost nobody enters it, and the ones we do reach we cannot reach twice. Which makes the next question the important one: why?

Part I · What we built, and where it stopped

03Why it fails

The reason is the approach we took, not the targeting.

Four things are wrong, and none of them is a bug. They are consequences of building a pull product and then trying to push people into it.

1 · Menus, not demos

"Do you want to know?" and "do you want this or that?" tell the user a category of question they are allowed to ask. They never show the intelligence itself. The user has to imagine the value before they have seen any of it — and for a first-time buyer with no mental model of what a shopping assistant is, that is a very large ask.

2 · You miss it, you miss it

The nudge is spoken once, and then it is gone. Nothing persists on the app, nothing to scroll back to, no record that builds the belief that Vaani understands what you are doing. Capability discovery ends up depending on one exact moment — and the decay curve above is what that costs us.

3 · Voice is a weak medium for push

Phones stay muted or quiet and some handsets have a lower baseline. Dense, shared households make audio easy to lose and socially awkward to use. TTS quality undermines trust in the very thing being offered. Voice remains excellent once intent exists — it is poor at creating it.

4 · Vaani is not omnipresent

It is not woven into each surface's own objective, so users experience a separate feature bolted to the app rather than intelligence inside the shopping experience they are already having.

Two of the three problems with voice-as-push are the user's environment, not our technology. We cannot engineer them away — only design around them, and that points squarely at visual-first delivery with voice as reinforcement.

The structural read

Users have low comprehension of what Vaani can do, and Vaani is pull-based until they already know what to ask. A pull product cannot teach its own capabilities to someone who does not know what to pull. Users do not know what they do not know — and no amount of nudge-copy optimisation solves that.

Underneath all four sits one universal lens

These four failures are specific to Vaani, but the friction they create is not. Every capability in any product is blocked by one of two things — and naming them gives us the frame we use for the rest of this document.

Not knowing — our job: show the path

They can't want what they can't see

The user doesn't know a capability exists, so they never reach for it.

e.g. The feed can be narrowed with filters — but the user has never discovered that filters exist.

→ Surface the capability at the exact moment it becomes useful.

Not expressing — our job: shorten the path

They know, but can't operate it

The user knows the capability exists but can't work it efficiently enough to reach their goal.

e.g. They know filters exist — but can't combine the right ones to land on what they have in mind.

→ Deliver the outcome without demanding mastery of the mechanics.

Both are measurable in our own data. Not knowing is everyone in the first bar of the funnel above — people who never reached for a capability they did not know was there. Not expressing is visible in every search box: before a first search shoppers click through a mean of 137 products, then two-thirds of them type a query with no attribute at all.

Two frictions, two jobs. Show the path, and shorten the path. Everything proposed in the second half of this document is one or the other — and intelligence is the first tool we have that can solve both at once.

04Yet habit proves trust can form

The product does not need endless prompting. It needs a better first outcome.

Everything so far has been about why people never start. This is the counterweight, and it matters just as much: the users who do get through behave exactly as we would want them to.

68%

89.6%

64.6%

84.0%

The first three say habit, once formed, is self-sustaining — those users stop needing us to prompt them at all. The fourth says what forms it. We took every user's first turn of a given ask, graded that first outcome, and checked whether they ever came back and asked the same thing again.

Density beats channel

Deep users had 16.7 vs 5.9 searches and 9.1 vs 2.4 turns in their first session. Search-led first sessions bounced at 41.7% vs 60.2%. A dense, productive first session is the predictor.

Channel barely matters

Nudge-first and organic-first users form habit at 7.0% vs 6.2%. How they arrived was never the difference. What happened once they got there was.

Order impact is real

Vaani users order more, and a positive gap survives even after tight matching on how often people open the app. The effect is genuine. What it costs to produce is the part worth looking at closely.

The gain is real. It is also expensive.

If a good outcome is what earns the next session, then the price of producing one is the honest measure of how good our outcomes are today. Two reads, both matched against comparable never-users.

Effort going up alongside orders going up is not a contradiction. It is what happens when an assistant helps you keep looking instead of helping you decide. Every turn that ends in a spoken answer sends the user back to browsing, and browsing is what the effort numbers are counting.

Which is exactly the loop we want, working — and where it breaks

Put those findings together and they describe a single mechanism. Vaani only earns a second use if the outcome was trustworthy — so the loop's first node and its third node have to carry the same currency: outcomes.

The read

Habit is not a motivation problem and it is not an acquisition-channel problem. It is an outcome-quality problem — at first contact, and at every contact after. Fix what happens in session one and the loop turns on its own.

Part II · What people actually do with it

05What habitual usage reveals

Vaani is not one universal voice behaviour. The job changes with the mission and the real estate.

Now that we know habit can form, the next question is what those users are actually doing — because that tells us whether repeat usage means the shopping problem is being solved.

FIND is not simply "search for a category" either. Most of what users say already carries usable constraints:

The habit hardens in one direction too. Between a user's first Vaani session and their tenth, DECIDE grows from 27.3% to about 32% of turns, and price grows from 60.0% to 66.5% of DECIDE. Power users skip the curve entirely — price is already 64.6% of their DECIDE turns in session one.

All of which maps onto five mission states

Part II · What people actually do with it

06The second ceiling — outcome quality

Solving adoption alone would still not be enough.

Suppose every user adopted Vaani tomorrow. The product they arrived at would still be bounded by the app it sits on — because if an answer is not already a feed, a product module, a search result or an app action, Vaani has nowhere to put it.

That ordering is the whole diagnosis: do the thing > answer specifically > answer generically > ask the user to try again. Our largest ask by volume is price, which today we can only answer with words — so it lands at 29.6%. And the ask that would actually resolve a price question, comparison, is the one nobody knows to make.

The deciding journey is where the value is, and where we are weakest

So the behaviour that produces most of our orders is comparison — done manually, by re-opening products one at a time. And when Vaani tries to help at that moment, the only tool it has is a fresh search, which makes things worse.

There is no place in the current product to hold "keep this, change that." Alternate is not "more like this" — it is preserve what the user likes, change what they do not. That is a state-management problem before it is a retrieval problem, and state is exactly what a feed cannot hold.

Everything we have built makes the app easier to operate. It does not yet make intelligence itself a way to shop. Vaani is good at operating an existing construct. It is weak the moment the answer requires reasoning, state, comparison, or an interface the app does not have.

Part III · The two fixes

Part III · The two fixes

09Fix two — the primary surface

Not a chat screen — a generative shopping interface.

Users already know how to browse. The goal is not to make every interaction conversational. Let them browse naturally until intelligence can materially improve the journey — then take real control.

☰
Browse naturally
The app exactly as it is today. If it isn't broken, don't fix it.
→
⚑
Find an anchor
A product close to what they want. Intent direction becomes clear.
→
✦
Vaani takes over the mission
Evaluate · Compare · Alternate · Decide

"Browse → find anchor" becomes "anchor → understand intent → Vaani actively completes the mission."

Voice in, anything out. Input stays conversational because it lets users express complex intent cheaply. Output is whatever the decision needs — cards, comparisons, filters, product sets, infographics, review synthesis, size visualisation, generated UI. Conversational input does not require conversational output.

That single separation also disarms the third adoption failure from §03. Low volume, shared households and weak TTS all stop being load-bearing the moment the answer is something you can see.

A two-way window

Meesho → user

Show them the future

Expose capabilities far richer than the current app supports: personalised comparisons, anchor-preserving alternatives, review synthesis, size visualisation — UI generated per mission rather than forced into one fixed layout.

User → Meesho

Let them tell us what it should be

An unconstrained interface reveals what users actually expect: what to automate, where the app fails, which decisions they struggle with. Every session is a discovery mechanism for the future product.

We use the primary surface to show users the future of Meesho — while users use it to tell us what that future should be.

Four rules the handoff has to obey

Carry complete context

Surface, product, prior behaviour, the verdict shown and the action taken all travel into primary.

Never open an empty Vaani screen

The intervention is Turn Zero; the tap is Turn One. The user's first words should be a refinement.

Never ask the user to restate

Not the product, not the constraint, not the mission. If Vaani had it on screen, it keeps it.

Preserve the anchor, and the way back

Easy return to the original product and to normal browsing. We do not trap the journey.

Every use improves the next.

The two surfaces joined by Turn Zero are not two features. They are a wheel — and the reason to build both is that neither turns on its own.

Step four is not theoretical. We can already see what a conversation knows that click data cannot reconstruct. Across the 40 heaviest Vaani users and the 29,317 turns between them, voice corrected, contradicted or wholly supplied what the platform believed for 26 of the 40 — and 8 of them are invisible in our order systems entirely.

Five phases from feature to interface

Not a transition we force in one release. We teach the interaction model through demonstrated utility, and Vaani's prominence grows only where users show pull.

1

Evidence

Vaani leaves visible proof of intelligence across feed, PDP, cart, wishlist and orders. No conversation required anywhere.

2

Interactive

Every intervention can be acted on — "yes, show more", "why?", "no, correct that". Vaani starts the conversation instead of waiting for it.

3

Turn Zero

The tap opens the primary surface mid-conversation, exactly as §10 describes.

4

Expand based on pull

Users who repeatedly engage receive richer outputs, more prominence and lower friction into primary Vaani. Prominence follows demonstrated demand.

5

Adaptive Meesho

The user states the outcome. Vaani orchestrates the experience — search, ranking, evidence, comparison and action — while Meesho provides the visual control surface.

The end state is not "Vaani replaces the app." Visual browsing remains valuable. The shift is that the user no longer needs to manually operate every interface to get an outcome — intelligence and UI reorganise themselves around the mission. Over time the question "is this Vaani or Meesho?" stops mattering.
Vaani becomes the intelligence layer through which Meesho adapts itself around what each user is trying to accomplish.

Part IV · Where it ends

12What we do next

Build somewhere worth going, before building the roads to it.

One primary-surface test that proves intelligence can produce a better outcome. Three secondary interventions that test whether visible intelligence creates pull. Then, and only then, join them.

Sequence: build a strong primary outcome → test a few high-utility secondary interventions → connect them through Turn Zero → scale only what proves useful. That is stronger than building broad secondary presence before there is somewhere valuable for the pull to go.

First: the primary-surface test — alternate discovery and comparison, on a price turn

The logic chains straight out of Part II. Price is the largest DECIDE ask. Price questions are usually unresolved decision asks, not discount requests. A broad re-search loses the anchor and the constraint. And revisit behaviour tells us comparison is what high intent looks like. So the first test is the one that puts all four together.

1
Anchor productThe item the shopper is on, pinned and kept visible throughout
↓
2
What must remainDesign, fabric, colour, size — whatever they liked, held constant
↓
3
What must changeThe failing constraint, captured from the price turn itself
↓
4
5–10 curated alternativesA small, reranked set — not another broad feed
↓
5
Explain why each qualifies"Same design, lower price." "Same look, better size availability."
↓
6
Compare, return to anchor, or proceedSide-by-side on the dimensions that matter, and a one-tap way back

That flow is the primary-surface thesis in its smallest testable form: an anchor, a preserved constraint, a generated set, and a reason for each item. If it works, "task first, UI second" stops being a principle and becomes a shipped behaviour.

Full scope and measurement: KRD — Alternative Discovery on Price Turns ↗

In parallel: three secondary-surface conviction tests

The smallest number of interventions that can tell us whether visible intelligence creates pull. Each one has to be useful on its own terms, whether or not it ever leads to Vaani. Three, not omnipresence.

PDP · decision evidence

"Buyers like the fabric, but sizing is inconsistent."

Targets the price ask we currently answer with words — 29.6% success today.

Wishlist / revisit · alternate trigger

"Vaani found a cheaper alternative to this."

Targets the revisit pattern that produces 55% of orders.

Feed · refinement verdict

"Vaani found 14 cotton kurtis under ₹500 in these results."

Targets the silent stretch before a first search.
