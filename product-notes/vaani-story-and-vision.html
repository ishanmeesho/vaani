Vaani · Product Future State · August 2026

From a voice feature to an intelligent shopping layer

What we built, how people actually use Vaani, why first-time adoption has a ceiling, and what the product should become next.

2.20Mclassified Vaani turns, 167,082 users
58.6%FIND — show me something
29.9%DECIDE — help me decide
6.5%of adopters reach 5+ sessions
87.4%of exposed users never adopt at all
Vaani 1.0 proved that intelligence can make Meesho easier to use. The next chapter is to make intelligence itself a way to shop.

Every number in this document is traceable to a dashboard linked in §15. Where a finding is observational rather than causal, it says so at the point of the claim.

00The story in one page

Users are already pulling Vaani into real shopping jobs. The product still makes them discover the AI before they can see its value.

Three beats carry this whole document.

1 · Users took Vaani further than we designed for

They do not treat it as a chatbot. 58.6% of turns are FIND and 29.9% are DECIDE — the job moves from "show me" to "help me decide." Price alone is 19% of everything anyone says to Vaani. The demand for decision help is already here.

2 · The ceiling is architecture, not adoption

Even at 100% adoption, today's Vaani is bounded by what the existing app can already show or do. Heavy users are not shopping with less effort — they click more per order, not fewer. That is a design limit, not a usage limit.

3 · Show intelligence, then let adoption follow

Secondary surfaces make useful intelligence visible inside Meesho before the user asks. Primary surfaces take over when the mission needs reasoning the app cannot express. Over time the boundary blurs.

The supporting findings, in order

The job changes by surface. Home and search are mission launch and refinement; PDP becomes a decision desk around price, quality, fit and pack.

Habit is real but narrow. 64.5% of adopters are one-and-done; 6.5% reach five or more sessions. Once they do, usage becomes mostly self-initiated.

A useful first outcome beats repeated prompting. Price repeats after a good first outcome 84.0% of the time, versus 62.8% after a bad one.

Pointed nudges prove a need moment, not a discovery model. Strict adoption is 12.9–14.1%, and roughly 61% of raw mic taps never become a real turn.

The shift: from "Vaani is something users invoke" to "Vaani understands the shopping mission and makes relevant intelligence continuously discoverable."
01Where we started · voice-first, thin UX

The original bet was intentionally narrow: keep the interface familiar, add intelligence through voice.

That was the right way to start. It let us learn how users behave with conversational help before rebuilding shopping around AI.

What this made possible

Low-effort expression for users who prefer speaking, struggle with spelling, or can state a need more easily than they can translate it into app taxonomy. Natural-language search, refinement and product questions.

What it did not change

The app still decided which surfaces, modules and outcomes existed. Vaani could operate those surfaces better, but it could rarely create a new experience around the user's mission.

“Spelling nahi aati hai toh … bol karke phir aa jaati hai.”

Qualitative research; user wording lightly punctuated.

One thing we did not plan for: a new modality surfaced demand that would never have appeared in the old construct. The clearest case is price negotiation — people started haggling with Vaani, at volume, because speaking made it possible. That is early evidence that new ways to engage produce new opportunities, and it is the seed of everything in Part II.

The funnel above is drawn on the 97,646 people who did adopt. The fourth tile is the denominator behind it: across the pooled experiments, 87.4% of Test-arm users — 5.07 million people who could have used Vaani — never spoke to it once. Both numbers matter: the habit is genuine, and it is being formed by a very small slice of the exposed base.

Once a user reaches five sessions the behaviour looks self-sustaining: 68% continue to another Vaani session, 89.6% self-initiate, and 64.6% use Vaani within their next five app opens. Habit is not the problem. Reaching it is.

What predicts depth is the quality of the first session, not the acquisition channel

Density beats channel

Search-led first sessions are denser and bounce less: deep users had 16.7 vs 5.9 searches and 9.1 vs 2.4 turns in their first session; search-led first sessions bounced at 41.7% vs 60.2%.

Channel barely matters

Nudge-first and organic-first users form habit at similar rates — 7.0% vs 6.2%. The difference was never acquisition. It was what happened after adoption.

Repeat can be friction

Price is highly repetitive — 8.7 turns/user, 35% do six or more — but only 29.6% successful overall. Repetition without resolution is not a habit; it is an unanswered question.

The loop is: right intervention → useful outcome → trust → voluntary repeat. More nudges are not the loop.

"Good" is source-defined downstream mission progress, not the same as order rate.

Why this still does not scale first-time adoption

Menus, not demos

Most nudges tell users a category of question they could ask. They almost never show the intelligence itself.

"You miss it, you miss it"

A transient voice nudge makes capability discovery depend on the exact moment. If it disappears, so does the lesson — and the decay curve above is what that costs.

Voice is weak for push

Low volume, shared households and TTS quality make voice a poor medium for proactive discovery. It stays excellent once intent exists.

Vaani is not omnipresent

It is not woven into each real estate's objective, so users see a separate feature rather than intelligence inside the shopping experience.

The first-time problem is structural: users have low comprehension of what Vaani can do, and Vaani is pull-based until the user already knows what to ask. Users do not know what they do not know.
06A bigger issue than adoption

Suppose adoption were 100%. Today's Vaani would still be bounded by the app we already built.

Direct action on FIND is genuinely strong. But the current product is limited to what existing Meesho constructs can already express — if the answer is not already a feed, a PDP module, a search result or an app action, Vaani has nowhere to put it.

And for the users who have adopted, shopping is not getting easier

The question changes from "How do we get more users to invoke Vaani?" to "Where should intelligence itself show up, and when should it own the experience?"
07The future product model · two surfaces, two jobs

Vaani needs two surfaces because it has two different jobs.

The distinction is not "small Vaani UI versus big Vaani UI." It is a division of responsibility. If the existing real estate can complete the task well, Vaani should assist it. If the task needs reasoning or an interface the app does not naturally have, Vaani should be able to own that part of the journey.

08Secondary surfaces · show value before asking for adoption

Show intelligence first, and let adoption happen as a consequence.

Today the first interaction asks the user to trust Vaani before Vaani has demonstrated anything. Secondary surfaces reverse the sequence: observe context → do something useful → attribute it to Vaani → let the user react → expand only if needed.

The empirical case: there is a long, silent, information-rich stretch of every session

Before a shopper's first search we already know a great deal about what they are doing, and we say nothing about any of it.

40.9% of searches land on a category the user had not browsed at all that session. A shopper who has clicked 50 products is telling us what they want; a one-word search is them failing to say it. That gap is exactly the not expressing problem — and it is the single largest surface for showing intelligence before adoption. 157,210 qualifying sessions · 25–27 Jul 2026

Any surface · one operating rule

Serve the real estate first. The user should get value even if they never open primary Vaani. If they want more, the secondary interaction becomes Turn 0 and carries context forward.

Secondary surfaces should not collapse into "nudges." They are a small set of reusable primitives that show up differently on each real estate.

Guardrail: omnipresent does not mean inescapable. If an intervention does not make the current real estate easier or more useful, it should not be there. This matters most for first-time and lower-confidence users — someone who feels ambushed does not complain, they just avoid.
09Primary surface · let intelligence own the outcome

Give Vaani room to own the outcome, not just answer inside the current app construct.

The primary surface is not "the Vaani chat screen." It is a Vaani-owned shopping surface for missions that need reasoning across products, signals or time — "same look but cheaper", "which of these should I buy?", "continue what I was looking at yesterday".

The behaviour that justifies it

Why a broad re-search is the wrong answer to it

Alternate is not "more like this." It is: preserve what the user likes, change what they do not. That is a state-management problem before it is a retrieval problem.
Proposed · not yet measured

What belongs on the primary surface

The core design principle is task first, UI second. Vaani should understand the mission and then render the interface that best solves it. Voice remains a strong input because it lets users express complex intent cheaply — but output should be visual whenever visual is clearer. Voice-first should never become voice-only, and that single distinction also disarms the third failure mode in §05.

MissionWhat Vaani should ownWhat the interface may need
AlternatePreserve what the user likes; change the failing constraint; retrieve a small better set.Pinned anchor, must-keep / must-change, 5–10 alternatives, reason labels, easy return.
CompareOrganise real candidates around the dimensions that matter for this mission.Side-by-side evidence, user-specific criteria, explicit trade-offs.
DecideSynthesize price, quality, reviews, seller, delivery, fit and user context.Verdict, evidence, uncertainty, follow-up actions.
ResumeCarry a past mission forward instead of restarting discovery.Previous anchors, expressed preferences, what changed, new candidates.

Concretely, the first version should: pin the anchor and keep it visible; extract "must keep" and "must change" from conversation and behaviour; show a small curated set of 5–10 rather than another broad feed; label why each item qualified; and support side-by-side comparison, shortlisting and one-tap return to the anchor.

10How the two connect · the secondary surface is Turn 0

Secondary and primary should behave like one continuous interaction.

A user should never have to restate context Vaani already had on screen. If Vaani says on a PDP "buyers like the fabric but sizing is inconsistent," and the user taps compare with the other one, primary should open already knowing the screen, the product, the previous product, the verdict, the behaviour and the action that triggered the transition.

The secondary interaction is Turn 0. The user's tap — "show me", "why?", "compare", "yes" — is Turn 1. Primary continues the same mission rather than opening with an empty prompt.

A worked example, shipping today: Amazon's price-history handoff

This is the whole model in two screens, in a shopping app, on the exact ask that dominates our own DECIDE branch. The user is on a product page looking at a price. Next to the price — in the product page's own idiom, not a chat bubble — sits a single link: Price history. Tapping it opens the assistant with the question already asked, and the answer comes back as a chart, not a paragraph.

It wears the surface's costume

On the PDP it is a text link beside a price. It is not a floating mic, a banner, or a miniature chat window pushed into someone else's screen. Our §08 design rule, shipped.

The entry point is the intent

Nobody taps "Price history" because they want to use an AI. They tap it because they want to know if ₹3,148 is a good price. Adoption is a by-product of answering the question they already had.

Voice in, visual out

The answer to "is this a good price" is a chart, not a sentence. That is §09's modality split working: conversational input does not require conversational output.

The Vaani version writes itself. Price is 19% of everything said to Vaani and 44.6% of those turns are negotiation. Today that ask gets a spoken price answer that succeeds 29.6% of the time, or a re-search that drops the constraint 85–95% of the time. The same handoff — a price affordance on the PDP, opening a Vaani surface that already knows the product and answers with evidence and alternatives — is exactly Bet 1 and Bet 2 in §13.

Behaviour can be the ask

Nobody typed a question. Ten views and no purchase is the question. Our equivalent is already the strongest signal we have: revisit sessions convert at 15.39% vs 3.12%, and buyers make 7.1 more same-type clicks after anchoring.

Showing the work builds the trust

"Reading 154k+ reviews" does more for confidence than the recommendation will. §04 is why this matters: a good first outcome takes price repeat from 62.8% to 84.0%. Legibility is how a first outcome earns belief.

It creates the ask we never get

Comparison is asked of Vaani 556 times out of 2.2M because nothing ever suggests it is possible. A strip of what you have been looking at, with one button above it, is how that demand becomes visible.

Two triggers, one architecture. The first example is entered through an intent the user already had; the second through a behaviour they did not know we could read. Both put the intelligence in the real estate's own idiom, both open primary with the context already loaded, and neither asks anyone to adopt an assistant. That is the whole of §07 through §10, shipping in a competitor's app today.

12Where this ends · one adaptive experience

The transition matters as much as the end-state.

We should not jump from today's Vaani to "Vaani becomes the app." The credible path has an intermediate state where app-first and intelligence-first journeys coexist, and Vaani's prominence grows only where users demonstrate pull.

13What we test next · small POCs to build conviction

Prove the architecture with a small number of high-intent, measurable bets.

Do not build the full vision at once. The next phase should prove three separate things: a Vaani-owned primary surface can create a better outcome than the current app construct; secondary surfaces can create pull without interrupting the journey; and context can pass between them without a reset.

Bet 1 · Alternate product discovery

Anchor product A. Capture explicit dissatisfaction. Preserve must-keep attributes, change the failing constraint, show 5–10 alternatives, label why each qualifies, and keep return to A trivial.

Bet 2 · Comparison

Compare products the user actually considered on price, reviews, seller, delivery, fit and stated constraints. Explain the trade-off; do not manufacture a generic winner. Today this is asked 556 times out of 2.2M — it is the largest unserved job we have.

Bet 3 · Secondary pull

Two or three high-confidence interventions on PDP, feed and wishlist. Show intelligence before asking for adoption. Measure both pull and interruption.

Bet 4 · Turn-0 handoff

Carry full surface, product, verdict, behaviour and trigger context into primary. The user should never have to repeat what Vaani already knew.

Sequence: make alternate discovery and comparison excellent on primary → test a few secondary interventions with strong local utility → connect them through Turn 0 → scale omnipresence only after value is proven.

The metric is not raw Vaani turns. The question is whether Vaani improved the mission, earned trust, and created later voluntary use.

The POCs are not the roadmap. They are conviction tests for one product primitive: anchor + constraint + action + memory. · Current alternate-product MVP definition ↗

14How we should measure Vaani

Measure mission progress, not conversation volume.

StageGood metricWhy
AdoptionShown → mic tap → real turn → productive actionA mic tap alone is not adoption — ~61% of them went nowhere
FINDOn-intent click, constraint applied, same-feed progressionA rendered search is only good if it acts on the mission
DECIDEGrounded answer → evidence / compare / save / ATC / orderA fluent factual answer is not enough
AlternateAnchor preserved, exact constraint carried, same-mission closureA category pivot is not success on the old mission
ContinuityShortlist, revisit, refind, resumeShopping is recursive, not linear
Long termOrganic self-initiation after a useful outcomeThe outcome should earn the next use
Data caveats we should keep visible
  • Turn-level "successful outcome" is a source-defined behavioural grading metric, not order rate.
  • Revisit and post-decision re-search associations are observational. The no-search comparison group is disproportionately made of conversations that were already going well. Use experiments to claim causality.
  • Strict-adopter cuts condition on the user completing the interaction; use randomised arm-level reads for incrementality.
  • Power-user cohorts are selected for high usage; their later-session behaviour should not be generalised causally.
  • Turns-per-incremental-order is an aggregate marginal-efficiency read across users, not a user funnel.
  • Windows differ by analysis and are stated on each chart. Numbers from different dashboards are directionally consistent, not arithmetic on one table.

Several figures here are worse than earlier versions of the same analysis, because the analyses were corrected rather than defended — nudge adoption roughly halved when we required a real conversation rather than a mic tap, and order uplift shrank by more than half when we matched properly on app-open frequency. Those corrections are the reason to trust the rest.
