Vaani product note · structured product thinking · August 2026

From invoked to reused

Adoption isn’t the goal. The goal is enough good outcomes that Vaani stops being something users are prompted to try and becomes something they choose to reuse.

This note is the framework underneath the other Vaani work — the loop we’re actually optimizing for, the five moments a shopping mission passes through, why nudging alone can’t carry adoption, and why behaviour has to become a first-class input alongside whatever the user explicitly says. It closes with one conceptual model that ties all of it together.

What this note is for. A structured distillation of whiteboard thinking on Vaani’s product direction — the mental model, not a spec or a build plan. It doesn’t cite usage numbers; for those, see How Vaani Can Help in Exploit Journeys and the dashboards it draws on. This note is about why the shape of the intervention matters, not what it converts today.
Mission — the shopping goal a user is pursuing, not any single query or turn.
Moment — where in the mission the user currently is: start, refine, alternate, decide, or resume.
Expressed intent — what the user explicitly tells Vaani, in words or taps.
Behavioural intent — what their clicking, revisiting and skipping implies, whether or not they’ve said it.
Intervention opportunity — the combination of need, intent, confidence and expected usefulness that decides whether Vaani should surface itself right now.
Habit loop — the cycle where a good outcome builds trust, and trust drives voluntary reuse without a nudge.

Part I

The loop we’re actually optimizing

Nudge → adoption → outcome → trust → repeat adoption. Two loops sit inside this chain, and only one of them is where the value actually compounds.

The immediate loop gets a single mission through Vaani once. The habit loop is the one worth designing for — it’s what turns Vaani from a feature a user was prompted into, into a habit they carry into the next session unprompted.

Adoption itself isn’t the end goal. The goal is to create enough successful outcomes that Vaani moves from something the user is prompted to use to something they choose to reuse.

Part II

The mission, not the turn

The unit to design for is a shopping mission, not a query. Within a mission, Vaani can help at five distinct moments — each with a different user state and a different job.

1

Start

Has a need but hasn’t found a product yet.

“I have not seen any product yet.”

→
2

Refine

Has seen products but wants to narrow or change something.

“I am not yet seeing something I like.”

→
3

Alternate

Likes or is considering a product but wants another option.

“This is close, but show me something better.”

→
4

Decide

Has candidate product(s), needs confidence to act.

“Should I buy this? Which one is better?”

→
5

Resume

Returns to an earlier mission or product, often a new session.

“Take me back to what I was considering.”

Resume is slightly different. It can happen in a brand-new session and should use previous visits, expressed preferences, and previously seen alternatives to restore the mission — rather than starting the user from zero.

Part III

The first-time adoption problem

Vaani is largely pull-based today: a user has to already know they can ask something before they use it. That creates a discovery problem underneath the conversion problem.

Users have low comprehension of Vaani’s capabilities — and users don’t know what they don’t know. A nudge that only tries to convert is solving half the problem.

01

Nudges have two jobs, not one

Every nudge is simultaneously a conversion attempt and a lesson in what Vaani can do. Framed only as the first, it under-delivers on the second.

job 1 · create adoption job 2 · teach the capability
Decision“Do you want to know whether this is worth buying?”
Refinement“Do you want something like this but cheaper?”
Alternative“Want to see similar options?”

None of these read as a generic sales prompt. Each one names a specific capability while making the offer — so even a user who declines walks away knowing something new about what Vaani can do next time.

Part IV

Why nudging alone can’t carry this

Three structural reasons a purely nudge-driven model of adoption runs into a ceiling.

A

Nudges are transient

“You miss it, you miss it.” The user may have a genuinely relevant need, but if the nudge appears at the wrong moment or disappears, the opportunity is gone.

The cost: a timing dependency that shouldn’t have to exist at all.

B

Voice isn’t a good medium to push

Voice works well once a user has decided to interact, but it’s not the best mechanism for proactively communicating that help is available in the first place.

The cost: discovery of Vaani should be visual-first; interaction can follow, in voice or text.

C

Vaani isn’t omnipresent

Vaani isn’t tightly coupled to the user’s actual shopping state today — what they’re looking at, what they’re trying to achieve, where they’re stuck, what Vaani could do right now.

The cost: the intervention needs to be coupled to real shopping state + inferred intent + current objective, not shown on a fixed schedule.

Part V

When should Vaani intervene?

The direction is simple to state — high intent plus high confidence should surface Vaani proactively — but the right moment depends on the type of mission the user is in.

SAME SESSION

Interventions that fit mid-mission

  • Refinement
  • Alternative
  • Decision support
NEW SESSION

Interventions that restore a mission

  • Resume
  • Restart a previous mission
This means Vaani shouldn’t just ask “should I nudge?” It should reason: what is the user’s current mission state, how confident am I, and what is the most useful next action Vaani can offer right now?

Part VI

The refinement problem has two different shapes

“Help the user refine” sounds like one job. It’s actually two, and they need opposite solutions.

SHAPE 1

Doesn’t know what to ask

The user can visually tell the products aren’t right, but can’t articulate the attribute that’s wrong. Vaani’s job is to help discover the refinement dimension itself — not to wait for a query that names it.

Solved by Vaani proposing candidate attributes, not by better search.

SHAPE 2

Knows, but isn’t expressing it

The preference exists and shows up in behaviour, even though the user hasn’t verbalized it.

  • Repeatedly opening a certain silhouette
  • Consistently skipping expensive products
  • Revisiting one colour family
  • Viewing the same neckline or design repeatedly
  • Returning to a certain product archetype

Solved by reading behaviour as intent, not by asking the user to say it out loud.

SYNTHESIS

Two sources, one preference model

Expressed intent — what the user explicitly tells Vaani.

Behavioural intent — what their browsing, clicking, revisiting and comparing implies.

Vaani should combine both, not treat expressed intent as the only real signal.

Part VII

“Alternate” is a preserve-and-change problem

Showing more products is the easy version of this job. The powerful version turns a vague rejection into a structured preference.

An alternate request is rarely “more like this.” It’s a rejection of one attribute layered on top of approval of everything else — and the retrieval should reflect exactly that split.

WEAKER JOB

Show alternatives

  • “Here are similar products you might prefer.”
STRONGER JOB

Help the user express what should change

  • “What do you like about this one, and what would you change?”
An alternate isn’t simply “more like this.” It is preserve what the user likes + change what they don’t.

Part VIII

Behaviour has to become a first-class input

Three sources of context should jointly determine what assistance Vaani surfaces — not the explicit ask alone.

EXPLICIT

What the user says

  • User asks directly
  • States a preference
  • Rejects something
  • Specifies a constraint
BEHAVIOURAL

What the user does

  • Click, dwell, revisit
  • Compare, skip
  • Search or refine
  • ATC / remove
  • Return to a previous product
MISSION CONTEXT

Where the user is

  • Current surface
  • Products already seen
  • Current candidate products
  • Current + previous related sessions
  • Stage of the decision journey

Part IX

The product shift

The biggest idea across all of this is a shift in what Vaani fundamentally is to the user — without becoming an assistant that constantly interrupts them.

TODAY
“Vaani is something users invoke.”
→
THE SHIFT
“Vaani understands the shopping mission and makes relevant assistance continuously discoverable.”
Design principle. Persistent availability, selective intervention.
Design principle. Visual-first discovery → conversational interaction when useful.

Part X

The full conceptual model

Putting all of the above into one chain, from a user's mission down to Vaani becoming part of how they shop — and back around again.

Mission understanding feeds intent understanding, which feeds an intervention-opportunity estimate — the actual decision point for whether and how Vaani shows up. Everything after that is the immediate loop from Part I; the dashed return is the habit loop, feeding back into a better-understood mission next time.

The core loop, restated

The north-star loop is mission understanding → right intervention → useful outcome → trust → repeat use — not simply more nudges → more adoption. That distinction is the most important idea across all of this thinking: a nudge that fires more often but doesn’t understand the mission just burns trust faster.

Adoption isn’t the finish line

It’s the entry point to a habit loop that only forms if the outcome was actually good.

Nudges teach, not just convert

Every nudge is a capability-discovery mechanism first, a conversion attempt second.

Behaviour is a first-class signal

What a user does should carry as much weight as what a user says.

Discovery is visual; interaction is voice

Don’t ask voice to do a job it’s structurally bad at — proactively announcing itself.
