Vaani product note · price discovery · August 2026

When the ask is price, answer with value

A buyer asking “is this the best price?” is not asking for a discount. They are asking whether they should buy this one.

Today a price turn on the PDP gets a price answer — or a category re-dump. This note describes the alternative: Vaani takes the price ask as a signal to go find a better-value product of the same type, using the anchor's own image, the constraints the buyer already gave us, and a price band next to where they are standing. It reads the feed the app returns, forms a verdict, walks the buyer to it, and defends it.

What this note is for. To give a feel for the thing we could build — the turn shape, the pieces, and the app capabilities each piece needs. The problem is already documented in How Vaani Can Help in Exploit Journeys; it is summarised here in five lines, not re-argued. Everything below is a proposal, not a spec — numbers like k, m and bucket widths are starting points to tune.
Anchor — the PDP the buyer is standing on when the turn fires. Everything is measured against it.
Carry-over filter — an attribute the buyer spoke, typed or tapped earlier in this session, in this same product type.
Adjacent price band — the price filter immediately containing the anchor's price, not the cheapest one available.
Top-k / plus-m — the first k feed products Vaani reads to form a verdict, plus m more as the buyer scrolls.
Verdict — Vaani's judgement of the best alternative, plus a short varied slate around it.
Dynamic CLP — a Vaani-curated feed holding only the alternatives it stands behind.

The five lines of context

The demand is already arriving, and we answer it badly

From the exploit-journey analysis, across 879,571 Vaani PDP turns and all order-placing sessions.

  • Price is the ask. Price is roughly 70% of DECIDE turns at every hunt depth, and 44.6% of those price turns are outright negotiation — “make it cheaper.”
  • Buyers want a fair price, not the floor. The alternative they click before buying is priced above the eventual purchase about as often as below. They shop a band.
  • Missing the band is expensive. Landing at 2× the price finally bought costs +56% more browsing. An exact match still costs 7.1 further same-type clicks.
  • We drop what they told us. Fewer than 15% of Vaani re-searches carry the discussed constraint forward.
  • And re-dumping the category is the worst available move — a same-sub-category re-search converts worse than doing nothing at all.
So the product question is narrow: when a buyer raises price on an anchor, can Vaani come back — in one turn — with a specific, available, constraint-respecting product that is better value, and say why?

Part I

The turn, end to end

One price-discovery turn, from trigger to verdict to comparison. Six stages; the buyer sees three of them.

The whole middle of the pipeline is invisible to the buyer. They say one thing and get one answer back — the search, the filtering and the reading happen underneath. Everything after the verdict is conversational and optional.

01

Stage by stage

Each stage below names what it needs in and what it must produce out. The chips are the contract, informally stated — Part III turns them into app capabilities.

  1. Anchor lock — decide what “this product” means

    The turn fires on a PDP with a price intent: asking the price, asking whether it is a good price, or negotiating. Vaani freezes that PDP as the anchor and keeps it for the rest of the exchange — every later comparison is against it. If the buyer is scrolling the anchor's own recommendation feed when they ask for something else, the anchor is still the PDP, never a card in that feed.

    in: price-intent turn in: current PDP out: AnchorContext.anchor

  2. Query build — image, hard filters, adjacent band

    Vaani composes a visual search from three parts. The anchor's own catalog image, passed whole — not a screen capture. The carry-over filters: every non-visual attribute the buyer has established in this product type this session, whether spoken (“cotton, not polyester”), typed, or tapped (choosing size L on a variant selector). Those go on as hard filters, because visual similarity cannot see them. And a price band adjacent to the anchor — for a ₹289 product, under ₹299, not under ₹199.

    in: full catalog image in: session filter memory (this sscat) in: anchor price out: visual search request

  3. Feed load — let the app do what it is good at

    The app runs the search and renders the feed exactly as it would for any visual search, with the filters visibly applied so the buyer can see and edit them. Vaani does not build its own retrieval. One requirement: the feed Vaani reads must contain only purchasable products — in stock, servicable to this pincode. A verdict on something the buyer cannot buy is worse than no verdict.

    in: visual search request out: rendered feed out: applied-filter state

  4. Read k, then m — judgement over the actual feed

    Vaani reads the first k products the app decided to show — the same ones the buyer's thumb would reach — with their price, rating, rating count, review-image presence, seller, badges and combo structure. If the buyer keeps scrolling instead of waiting, Vaani reads m more and revises. Judgement is incremental and always reflects what the buyer can actually see.

    in: ordered top-k feed cards in: +m on scroll out: scored candidate set

  5. Verdict — name one, offer a few

    Vaani states the best alternative in one line, with the reason attached (“same fabric, 4.2 stars on 900+ ratings, ₹40 less”). Behind that single pick sits a short varied slate — not five near-identical cheapest items, but a spread along the axes buyers actually trade off: the best rating-for-price, the safest high-confidence pick, the cheapest that still clears the bar. If nothing in the feed genuinely beats the anchor, Vaani says so; “stay where you are” is a valid, trust-building verdict.

    in: scored candidates + anchor out: best pid + slate + reasons

  6. Deliver, then defend

    The best product is rarely at the top of the feed, so Vaani must be able to move the buyer to it: scroll the feed to that position, open a Vaani-curated feed holding just the slate, or open the winner's PDP directly. Then it offers the follow-up that does not exist today — “want to know which one to buy?” — and answers it by comparing candidates against each other and against the anchor on product, seller and platform-performance signals.

    in: verdict out: scroll-to / curated feed / open PDP out: comparison answer

03

The state Vaani has to hold

Most of the difficulty is not the model — it is remembering the right things across the session and across surfaces. One object, populated before the decision turn happens, not at it.

AnchorContext {
  anchor: {
    pid, catalog_id, sscat_id,
    image: // full catalog asset, not a screenshot
    price, rating, rating_count, has_review_images,
    seller_id, selected_variant, badges: [ mall? ],
    source: // how the buyer got here: search | widget | recs
  },

  filters: [                        // everything established in THIS sscat, this session
    { attr: "colour", value: "red",    source: "spoken",   turn: 3       },
    { attr: "fabric", value: "cotton", source: "spoken",   turn: 3       },
    { attr: "size",   value: "L",      source: "tapped",   pid: 88231    },
    { attr: "brand",  value: "...",    source: "searched", query_id: ... },
  ],

  price_rule: { anchor_price: 289, band: "under_299", strategy: "adjacent" },

  read: { k: 12, m_step: 10, read_pids: [...], scroll_depth: n },

  verdict: null | {
    best_pid, slate: [ { pid, why, axis } ], vs_anchor: { ... },
    revised_at_scroll_depth: n
  }
}
  • Filters are append-only within an sscat and scoped to it. A size tapped on kurtis must not travel to a search for shoes.
  • Source matters. A tapped size is a harder constraint than a colour mentioned in passing; the strength affects whether it becomes a hard filter or a ranking preference.
  • The anchor survives navigation. The buyer can scroll the reco feed, open the curated feed, come back — the anchor only changes when they genuinely land on a new PDP and start a new hunt there.

Part II

Design rules learned the hard way

These come out of poking at the current app flow. Each one is a decision that looks arbitrary until you get it wrong.

R1

Pass the image, not the screen

Visual search must receive the anchor's whole catalog image. A screenshot of the PDP is not reliable input — it carries chrome, overlays, crops and compression, and the match quality drops noticeably.

Breaks as: plausible-looking but wrong-category results, and no way to debug why.

R2

Nearest price band, never the floor

Pick the price filter that just contains the anchor's price — ₹289 → under ₹299. Jumping to the cheapest available band is a bigger divergence than the buyer asked for.

Breaks as: a feed of low-quality catalogs where the relevant product never appears at all.

R3

The PDP is the anchor, not a reco card

If the buyer asks for an alternative while scrolling the anchor's recommendation feed, run the search on the PDP they came from — not on whichever product happens to be on screen.

Breaks as: silent anchor drift; comparisons against a product the buyer never chose.

R4

Read k first, then m more

Form a verdict from the top k so the answer is fast, and keep reading m more while the buyer scrolls. Judgement should deepen with their attention, not stall waiting for completeness.

Breaks as: either a slow turn, or a shallow verdict the buyer disproves by scrolling.

R5

Hold the anchor through the whole exchange

Every claim Vaani makes is relative — cheaper than what, better rated than what. The anchor's own attributes must be in memory for the comparison turn, not re-fetched or guessed.

Breaks as: vague verdicts — “this is good” instead of “₹40 less, 4× the ratings.”

R6

Carry every filter, however it arrived

Spoken, typed, tapped — all three are constraints. Non-visual attributes especially (colour, fabric, size, age, capacity) must go on as hard filters, because image similarity cannot enforce them.

Breaks as: the <15% carry-over failure we already measure — asking the buyer to repeat themselves. Deferred past Scope 3 (Part V) — scoping keeps only the price filter, plus the anchor's own selected variant from Scope 2.

R7

Be able to walk them to it

The best product is often mid-feed. A verdict Vaani cannot navigate to is just a claim. Scroll-to-position, curated feed, and open-PDP all need to be things Vaani can trigger.

Breaks as: the buyer hunting for the product Vaani just recommended.

R8

Available products only

Out-of-stock and non-servicable products are filtered before Vaani reads the feed, not after. Availability is part of retrieval, not a post-hoc apology.

Breaks as: a confident recommendation for something that cannot be bought.

R9

A varied slate, not the cheapest five

Buyers triangulate a fair price from both sides. Surface options spread across the trade-off — rating × price — and let the buyer pick their own point on it.

Breaks as: a cheapest-first list that reads as a downgrade, not a find.

R10

Do the value maths, including combos

If a 2-piece combo lands at a lower effective per-unit price, that is the better-value answer and Vaani should compute and say it. Same for free-delivery thresholds.

Breaks as: recommending a ₹249 single when a ₹449 pair was the actual win.

Explicitly not in scope. This is not a negotiation engine — Vaani does not change prices or promise discounts. It is not a replacement for search or for the ranking stack; it composes a query, reads what the app returns, and adds judgement on top. And it is not a cheapest-finder: the goal is better value at a comparable price point, which is what buyers are demonstrably doing by hand.

Part III

What the app has to hand Vaani

The interesting build work is the seam between the assistant and the app. Roughly seven capabilities, each independently useful.

CapabilityDirectionWhat it carriesWhy it is needed
Anchor snapshot app → Vaani pid, catalog id, sscat, full catalog image reference, price, rating, rating count, review-image flag, seller, selected variant, badges, arrival surface Everything downstream is relative to the anchor (R1, R5).
Session filter memory app → Vaani Every attribute set in this sscat this session, each with value, source (spoken / typed / tapped) and where it came from The carry-over gap is the single highest-leverage fix (R6).
Visual search invocation Vaani → app Search by image reference + hard attribute filters + price band + availability constraint, rendered as a normal, user-editable feed Vaani composes the query; the app owns retrieval and ranking (R2, R8).
Feed read-back app → Vaani Ordered top-k cards with pid, price, rating, rating count, review images, seller, badges, combo structure, availability — then m more on scroll Vaani must judge what the buyer is actually shown, in the order shown (R4).
Feed navigation control Vaani → app Scroll to a feed position, open a PDP, open a Vaani-curated feed built from an explicit pid list Out of scope 1–3. Reranking puts the answer at the top instead, and the buyer opens products themselves.
Comparison data fetch app → Vaani For a small pid set: seller quality and return/cancellation behaviour, catalog-level platform performance, price history/stability, combo and delivery economics Powers the verdict — and already exists as the P1/P2 enrichment fields (Part V), so this is a read, not a new build.
Filter surface sync Vaani → app The filters Vaani applied are visible and editable in the feed UI; buyer edits flow back into filter memory Trust. The buyer must see — and be able to loosen — what was assumed on their behalf.
The read-back is the piece worth designing carefully. “Give Vaani the top k cards the app decided to show, in order, with their commercial attributes” is the capability that unlocks judgement over any feed — not just this turn. Once it exists, Vaani can form an opinion about a search feed, a CLP, a reco rail or a curated slate with the same machinery.

Part IV

Where value hides

Two extensions that fall out of the same pipeline once judgement over a feed exists.

04

Combo maths

A combo catalog can beat a single-unit listing on effective price, and no buyer computes this reliably while scrolling.

If the feed contains a 2-piece or 3-piece pack whose per-unit price undercuts every single listing — and the buyer's stated constraints still hold across the pack — that is the better-value answer, and Vaani should say it in per-unit terms: “₹449 for two, so ₹225 each — cheaper than the ₹249 single.” The same arithmetic covers free-delivery thresholds and any per-unit vs. per-pack framing. This needs combo structure in the feed read-back, and a per-unit normalisation step before scoring.

05

Mall cross-sell where trust dominates price

In some categories the right answer to “is this cheap enough?” is “spend slightly more and be sure.”

Health and appliances are the clear cases: the cost of a bad unit is far higher than the price gap, so a Mall product a little above the anchor's band can be the genuinely better-value pick. Vaani should be able to break its own adjacent-band rule upward in these categories, and must say why when it does — the recommendation only works if the trust reason is stated. This needs category-level eligibility and Mall badges in the feed read-back, and should be gated to categories where we can defend the trade-off.

Part V

Three scopes, from least effort up

From the tech discussion. Parts I–III describe the full-fat turn; these are the three ways it can actually land, smallest first. Each is shippable on its own, and each one keeps the same trigger — a price ask or a negotiation on the PDP — and the same first move: an image search with a price filter applied.

SCOPE 1

Rerank the feed. No LLM.

The price turn fires the image search with its price filter. The backend takes the top 50 candidates from the already-filtered set and reorders them with hand-written rules before the feed renders, so the good products land at the top of what the buyer sees.

  • Rules read P0 signals only — price, rating, rating count, seller rating, availability, Mall/Gold
  • Fewer than 4 candidates survive the filter → no rerank. The feed goes out in its default order
  • A candidate does not have to beat the anchor to rank — this stage only produces an ordered list
  • Silent. Existing image-search feed, reordered, no copy and no new surface

Cheapest path: backend-only, no Android or design work. Reranking also removes the need to walk the buyer anywhere — if the right product is at rank 1, nothing has to scroll to rank 7.

SCOPE 2

Speak the verdict.

Same reranked feed. The top 6–7 products go to an LLM with their product IDs, and it decides which of them are worth talking about. Vaani says it out loud. This is where the anchor finally enters — the verdict compares it against the ranked products.

  • No fixed k. The model gets a 6–7 product window and picks what to mention; a prompt governs what counts as worth saying
  • Adds P1 (review images present) and P2 (return rate, seller tenure, delivery promise) on top of P0 — already available from the information-enrichment work
  • The anchor's selected variant (a size tapped on the PDP itself) is in scope here, for the comparison
  • Voice only. No deep-link, no scroll-to — the buyer opens the product themselves

Ranking stays deterministic; the model only explains and compares. Easier to trust, and cheap to debug when a verdict reads wrong.

SCOPE 3

An SDUI custom CLP.

The top 50 go to the LLM, which sorts them into groups from a taxonomy we define and instruct — not invented per query. The CLP opens every time, and is SDUI-driven, so the backend can place whatever content it needs into it.

  • Groups: arrives faster · better quality · low return risk · experienced seller · absolutely cheapest · has real review images
  • Can pin the anchor product, and is the real estate comparison eventually lives in
  • Source-agnostic: image search becomes just one source into the CLP; the reco feed is another
  • If nothing in the alternatives is meaningful, Vaani can say so and offer reco-sourced suggestions instead

The most useful and the most expensive — the one that needs Android and design work. The SDUI surface is the durable asset here, not this one flow.

06

Signals, and which scope can see them

Settled with tech. The tiering is the real constraint on Scope 1 — the rerank rules can only be as good as P0.

TierSignalsRead byNote
P0 Price · rating · rating count · seller rating · availability · Mall / Gold flag backend rerank + LLM verdict The only signals Scope 1 can rank on. Availability is a gate, not a ranking term.
P1 Review images present or not LLM verdict Already enriched. Note the tension: this is the most lopsided signal in the buyer data (bought products almost always have review images) and the rerank cannot see it.
P2 Return rate · seller tenure · delivery promise LLM verdict Already enriched. These are what make a verdict sound like advice rather than a price readout.
The six Scope 3 groups map one-to-one onto these tiers — cheapest and better quality from P0, real review images from P1, arrives faster / low return risk / experienced seller from P2. The taxonomy needs no data we do not already have.

07

Settled mechanics

  • Candidate pool: top 50, taken after the price filter is applied.
  • Rerank floor: under 4 surviving candidates, do not rerank at all.
  • Anchor: irrelevant to ranking (Scope 1); central to the verdict (Scope 2).
  • Spoken set: a 6–7 product window in, model-chosen subset out, prompt-governed.
  • Output channel: voice. No deep-links, no scroll-to, no Vaani-driven navigation at all.
  • CLP: opens on every turn, SDUI-driven, source-agnostic.
  • Mall cross-sell: in.
  • Carry-over filters: out, except the anchor's own selected variant in Scope 2.
Deliberately out of scope. Carry-over filters — replaying the colour, fabric or size the buyer established earlier in the session (R6, and the <15% gap in Part 0) — are held back for a later scope. Scopes 1–3 carry only the price filter, with one exception: the variant already selected on the anchor PDP itself is in from Scope 2, since it sits on the anchor rather than in session memory. Read Parts I–III with that in mind — the anchored search here is visual-plus-price, not visual-plus-everything-they-said.
What moves, relative to Parts I–III. The pipeline earlier in this note assumes Vaani reads a feed and forms its own verdict. Under this scoping the choosing moves into deterministic backend logic, and the model's job shrinks to explaining that choice (Scope 2) or grouping the candidate pool against a taxonomy we set (Scope 3). Two capabilities in Part III are struck rather than deferred: feed navigation control is out entirely — the buyer opens products themselves — and comparison data is not a new fetch, it is the P1/P2 enrichment that already exists. And by Scope 3 the CLP, not the image-search feed, is the surface: image search becomes a source feeding it, alongside reco.

08

How we would know it worked

Judged on effort and same-type conversion — never on within-Vaani order rate, which is selection rather than lift.

  • Constraint carry-over rate — share of price turns whose search preserved the stated attributes and a sane price band. Today under 15%; this should be near-total by construction.
  • Clicks to decision after the turn — the exploit grind is 7.1 same-type clicks after the anchor. A working alternative engine cuts it.
  • Verdict follow-through — how often the buyer accepts the walk-me-there, and how often the product they buy is the one Vaani named.
  • Comparison turn uptake — whether “which one should I buy?” gets asked once it becomes answerable.
  • Text-search fallback — how often the buyer abandons Vaani for raw search after the turn. Down is good.

09

Open questions

  • When is a price turn a negotiation and nothing else? Some buyers want a coupon, not an alternative. Does the alternative land as the first answer, or after a short acknowledgement?
  • How hard is a hard filter? If hard filters empty the feed, which one loosens first — and does Vaani say that it loosened it?
  • What are k and m? Traded off against latency and cost per turn; needs measurement, not a guess.
  • Band edges. What happens at the top of the bucket ladder, or when the anchor sits exactly on a boundary?
  • Scroll-to vs. curated feed. Which delivery reads as more helpful — showing the winner in context, or a clean slate of Vaani's picks?
  • Where the verdict is “stay put.” Saying “this is already the right buy” may be the highest-trust outcome available; it needs its own bar.

The shape of it, in one paragraph

A buyer standing on a product asks about price. Vaani takes that as the cue to go looking — using the product's own image, everything the buyer has already told us, and a price band right next to where they are. The app returns a feed; Vaani reads the top of it, decides what the best available option actually is, says so with a reason, walks the buyer there, and then answers the question nobody can answer for them today: which of these should I buy?

One turn, not a mode

This lives inside the existing price turn on the PDP. Nothing new to discover, no new entry point to teach.

Judgement over the app's own feed

We do not rebuild retrieval. We compose a better query and add an opinion on top of what the app already ranks.

Memory is the feature

Most of the perceived intelligence comes from carrying constraints and the anchor forward — not from a smarter model.

The read-back generalises

Once Vaani can read a feed, it can hold an opinion about any feed. That capability outlives this note.

Status. Product note — a proposal for discussion, not a committed spec. Flow, thresholds and capability boundaries are all open.

Sources. Behavioural framing and all cited figures come from How Vaani Can Help in Exploit Journeys (Vaani PDP turns and order-placing sessions, Aug 2026). The app-flow learnings in Part II come from hands-on exploration of the current Meesho app rather than from logged data.

Caveat. The report's cohort comparisons are descriptive and selection-shaped, not causal. The case for this build rests on the demand and the mechanism; the lift itself would be confirmed against a holdout.
